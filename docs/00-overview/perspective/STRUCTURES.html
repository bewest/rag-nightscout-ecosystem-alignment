    Who controls what: the ecosystem's control structures, today and proposed¶

    Living document, last changed 2026-10-19 at 35ec9da5a.

    What this is: a map (part 2 of the overview, perspective; how the overview is organised), for the Nightscout Foundation board, the maintainers of the ecosystem projects, and the community. Read after accountability thresholds; leads to process models. Living page, written 2026-10-16, relabelled 2026-10-17; 1·agents listed 2026-10-18. Drawings to follow and discuss the overview's proposals with: who controls whom, what comes back, and where money flows. Every node and edge comes from a page linked under the drawing; nothing here is a new finding. The drawings marked proposed show a proposal for discussion, not a decision.

    Author's interests: the author maintains cgm-remote-monitor, founded a company that hosts Nightscout commercially, and could be a candidate for paid work under the related proposals. The collaboration rules §5 would apply to that company and to the author like anyone else. Operators in the drawings are roles, not named companies.

    How to read the drawings¶

    The drawings follow the convention of STPA (system-theoretic process analysis) control structures. Whoever controls sits above what they control. Control actions run down the left of each loop and the feedback that tells a controller what happened runs up the right. A loop with no feedback edge is a loop whose controller cannot see the result of what it does.

    An icon at the left of each box says what the box does: a factory for a manufacturer, a civic building for a legislature, scales for a regulator, one figure for a person and two for people, a key for a credential authority, a phone for an app. The key lists every icon. The icons are from Lucide (ISC licence), copied into tools/structure-diagrams/icons/ with their licence.

    Every drawing is labelled level · subject. The level is where the drawing sits in the hierarchy, from society down to software and devices; the subject is a short name that stays the same as drawings are added beside it.

    level what sits at it drawings
    0 · society legislatures, regulators and the sector around AID in general 0·sector; 0·diy-status*
    1 · ecosystem the ecosystem's organisations and how they connect 1·today, 1·proposed (and six stakeholder views); 1·authority*, 1·data-rights*, 1·hosting*, 1·federation*, 1·teams*; 1·contract (drawn on the Conway's Law case study §3); 1·agents (drawn on agents in the ecosystem §1)
    2 · one organisation or agreement one entity, one agreement, one programme 2·agreement, 2·controller-operator; the precedents 2·sqlite, 2·cncf, 2·apple; 2·data-commons*, 2·shared-infra*
    3 · technical process one loop in software and devices 3·controller, 3·hub-sync, 3·delegation, 3·person-loop; 3·identity*, 3·registration*
    idea one idea from the argument, drawn small idea·sponsor-or-operate, idea·one-codebase, idea·same-requirements, idea·oversight-layer, idea·tiers, idea·credential-lifetime, idea·complaints, idea·designated-third-party

    * Drawn three times on one layout (believed, actual, and emerging or proposed) on the companion page Process models: what we believe, and how it works.

    A box marked [zoom: 2·agreement] is drawn in more detail in that drawing. Markers are used only where the link between two drawings would not be obvious otherwise: 0·sector's commons is the whole of 1·proposed; 1·proposed's credential authority is where the agreements of 2·agreement reach the projects; its regulated controller operator is 2·controller-operator; the people at the bottom of 1·proposed, and the controller app in 2·controller-operator, run the loop drawn in 3·controller. The levels are views of one system, not separate systems, so the same box can appear at several levels: the credential authority is in 1·proposed, 2·agreement and 3·person-loop.

    Drawings that span more than one kind of boundary carry horizontal bands: legal (law, regulation and the authorities that make them), social (organisations, agreements and people) and technical (software and devices). The bands show where accountability crosses from one to the next.

    0·sector¶

    The two columns follow Leveson's sociotechnical form: development on the left, operations on the right. The commons sits between them because it is both: specifications and release processes on one side, documentation and hubs that people use on the other.

    1·today¶

    Sources: how decisions are made today §1, §3 and §4; partner readiness §1 and §3. What the drawing shows by its gaps:

    • No edge joins a project or the foundation to a manufacturer. Projects reach devices through protocols volunteers worked out (a data edge, not an agreement).
    • No complaint or incident route leaves the ecosystem. Feedback from people reaches projects as issues and logs, and stops there.
    • The only funding edges are the foundation's support to projects and subscriptions to hosted providers.

    1·proposed¶

    Sources: ecosystem programme §2b (the foundation sponsors by default and operates only by recorded decision); partner readiness §2 (tiers A, B and C), §6 (complaint intake) and §7 (registry, credential authority and hosting kept apart); precedents (Tidepool Loop: a regulated track beside the DIY one).

    What changes against today: each new edge to a manufacturer runs through a named legal entity. The foundation signs for tier A, observe and export. Tier B and C relationships go to an operator with its own contract, insurance and successor: a credential authority, or a regulated controller operator as Tidepool was for Loop. The DIY track keeps its own edges to people. Complaints and incidents gain a route back to the manufacturer; that route is drawn one level down, in 2·agreement, and the person's own loop in 3·controller, so that this drawing carries one idea: who holds which agreement. The manufacturer is drawn above the foundation because in this drawing it grants access; that is the direction of control, not a ranking.

    The same drawing, from each stakeholder's side¶

    Each view greys out everything that does not touch the stakeholder. The layout does not change, so the views can be laid side by side.

    view what this stakeholder controls what reaches them as feedback
    a person with diabetes or a caregiver which releases, hosted hubs and devices they use, and whether to pay a host releases, hosted hubs and a cleared app; their complaints and their own loop are in 2·agreement and 3·controller
    a project maintainer releases to people conformance results go up; credentials and tests come down from bodies the project does not run
    a Nightscout-as-a-service operator the hosted hubs subscriptions; hub conformance is proposed, not existing
    a manufacturer agreements, credential authority, clearance submissions a readiness dossier from the foundation and submissions from any regulated operator; forwarded complaints are in 2·agreement. None of this exists today
    the foundation board sponsorship of shared operations the tier A agreement and nothing else; it operates nothing in this drawing
    a regulator clearance of manufacturers and of any regulated controller operator adverse events and submissions; the DIY track has no edge to a regulator

    The "feedback" column is read from the drawing; it is where the stakeholders are likely to disagree, and where the drawing should be corrected if it is wrong.

    2·agreement¶

    Source: partner readiness §4 (terms that carry data rights), §6 (complaints in both directions) and §7 (three roles kept apart; separating request from effect). The registry says who qualifies, the credential authority issues credentials, and the client release is hosted or built by someone else again. The client's commands to the device are requests; what comes back is the state the device applied, the split the hub-sync architecture already makes.

    2·controller-operator: Loop-as-a-service¶

    A proposal for discussion. Sources: the special controls for interoperable automated glycemic controllers (iAGC, 21 CFR 862.1356), integrated CGMs (iCGM, 862.1355) and alternate controller enabled pumps (ACE, 880.5730), as summarised in the stack census cloud layer; history and precedents for Tidepool Loop, cleared as an iAGC on 2023-01-23 (K203689); partner readiness §2 (tier C) and §7.

    What makes this a different drawing from 2·agreement:

    • The operator is the legal manufacturer of the controller. It answers to the regulator for one validated release and its updates. The DIY track continues beside it, as DIY Loop did beside Tidepool Loop.
    • One codebase. The cleared release is a validated build of the same open-source releases, not a separate edition (programme, "One codebase, two uses": SQLite sells assurance on the releases free users get; CNCF certifies vendors against one upstream). Fixes and validation evidence from the regulated track return to the shared codebase, drawn as a proposed edge. The reason for the edge: without it the regulated track becomes a second upstream, and the open project and the operator compete for the same fixes and the same maintainers' time.
    • The interfaces are regulated on both sides. The iAGC controls ask for validated interface specifications, secure authentication (pairing) and a process for sharing and validating the interface with connected devices. The ACE pump and iCGM controls ask the device side for the same. The pump and CGM manufacturers' edges to the operator are therefore agreements, not protocols worked out by volunteers.
    • The controller's inputs and outputs are restricted. Under the iAGC regulation it takes glucose only from iCGMs and commands only ACE pumps, unless the regulator determines otherwise. An ACE pump must fall back safely when it loses the connection.
    • Feedback has a record. The regulation asks for a record of critical events. Together with complaints, it returns to the operator and from there to the regulator, which the DIY loop in 1·today does not have.

    The drawing does not settle who operates such an entity, whether the foundation sponsors it (programme §2b: sponsoring does not make the foundation the operator), or how the return edge works when a fix to the open codebase has not yet been validated for the cleared release. The regulatory summary above paraphrases the regulations; it is for regulatory counsel to confirm against the current text and any device-specific clearance.

    The technical loops¶

    Contributor-facing in detail; summarised here for every reader. Three loops in software and devices, drawn from the source code of Loop, Trio and AndroidAPS (AAPS) and the Nightscout server. The detail, with file and line references, is in the technical loops note (read 2026-10-17 from the revisions it lists; read from the code, not run on a device). The design pages they serve embed the same drawings.

    3·controller¶

    The classic STPA loop: a controller (the app and its algorithm), an actuator (the pump), the controlled process (the body's glucose and insulin) and a sensor (the CGM), with the person supervising. All three apps command short temporary basal rates of up to 30 minutes, and automatic or manual boluses; they stop dosing when the newest glucose reading is too old (15 minutes in Loop, 12 in Trio, 7 in AAPS, each read from the code; Loop and Trio rechecked at LoopWorkspace v3.14.9 and Trio v1.0.2 on 2026-10-17). What each app does with a failed or uncertain pump command, and how the person can open or close the loop, differ; the note's §1.3 compares them. This is a description of how the software is built, not advice on using it; settings are for each person and their care team.

    3·hub-sync¶

    The hub in the middle: who writes each kind of record (glucose, treatments, device status, profiles) and who reads it. What a controller says it applied and what someone asked for are kept apart, the split the hub-sync architecture makes.

    3·delegation¶

    How a caregiver, or an automated agent acting for the person, sends a command (a remote bolus, carbs, a temporary target, an override) to the controller. Measured gaps (note §3): the hub keeps no record of a command with an id and an acknowledgement, except in AAPS's development branch; a reply reaches the sender only where the sender supplied a return path; "success" means the pump accepted the command, not that delivery finished; and an automated agent looks the same as whoever shared the credential with it. The proposed command record and acknowledgement are drawn in 3·registration, as proposed.

    3·person-loop¶

    Source: partner readiness §7, "no single point of failure for therapy". The credential authority is the only node above the person that can reach the loop, and the drawing puts it outside the loop: the app holds a grant that stays valid offline within bounds, so the authority being unreachable does not stop the loop. What the bounds are is for each manufacturer to define and validate; the drawing does not describe how any released system behaves.

    Single ideas¶

    Leveson's figures are crisp partly because each carries one idea. These drawings take one idea from the overview's argument and draw it with as few boxes as it needs, in two or three panels on one layout.

    idea·sponsor-or-operate¶

    The same service, drawn twice. The only change is who sits in the operator's box, and with it where uptime, incidents, complaints and claims arrive. Sponsoring passes money down and reports up; operating passes control down and makes every incident the foundation's. The programme proposes that the foundation sponsors by default and operates only by a recorded decision naming the operator, its contract, insurance and successor (programme §2b); the duties that operating brings are listed in what each activity brings §3.

    idea·one-codebase¶

    One project's releases serve both people who build their own and an operator, with no separate edition (programme, "One codebase, two uses"; SQLite's and CNCF's models in precedents). The second panel is what the proposed return edge prevents: a fork, after which the open project and the operator compete for the same fixes and the same maintainers' time (2·controller-operator).

    idea·same-requirements¶

    The requirements do not change with the kind of organisation; meeting them usually takes a legal entity. Evidence: the FDA routes are open to any manufacturer and Tidepool, a nonprofit, took the same 510(k) route; Dexcom's agreement is standard and non-negotiable; Washington's health-data act does not exclude nonprofits; Apple asks healthcare apps to come from a legal entity (what each activity brings §5).

    idea·oversight-layer¶

    A nonprofit's role is oversight: published criteria, listing and certification, applied the same way to every company, with results returned in public. Each company keeps its own legal and regulatory duties; the nonprofit does not take them over (CNCF, the Open Home Foundation, TEFCA and eduGAIN in precedents; what each activity brings §5).

    idea·tiers¶

    The same relationship three times, one agreement per tier, each adding lines: observe and export; configure and coordinate; control insulin delivery. The tiers are this repository's terms for technical risk, not FDA classifications; the recommendation is to ask for tier A first and separately (partner readiness §2).

    idea·credential-lifetime¶

    One credential in three states, each with a defined safe state. A credential service must not be able to stop insulin delivery by being unreachable; revocation follows a published process, and must leave a person's devices in a defined safe state (partner readiness §4 and §7). The bounds and safe states are for each manufacturer to define and validate; the drawing states requirements, not the behaviour of any released system.

    idea·complaints¶

    One intake for support, complaints and security, routing each report to the party legally responsible. It closes two loops: regression tests back to the projects, and referrals out to the manufacturer, whose own reporting duties stay its own (21 CFR Part 803). Intake is not legal responsibility (partner readiness §5 and §6).

    idea·designated-third-party¶

    In the EU since 2025-09-12, the person decides who receives their device data, and the data holder must send it there; the recipient is bound to the agreed purposes (vendor note §3.1). This is a draft summary of the law for counsel to review, not legal advice. The drawing is also embedded in DIGITAL-RIGHTS, beside the Data Act entry.

    What the drawings do not show¶

    • Legal relationships in detail. An edge labelled "agreement" stands for terms that partner readiness §4–§5 lists; which party is the manufacturer for which function, liability and intellectual property are for counsel and are not drawn.
    • Sizes and amounts. Edges show that money flows, not how much.
    • Every participant. Clinicians appear only in 0·sector; the support community and hubs other than Nightscout are not drawn.

    Changing a drawing¶

    The drawings are generated. Each one's source is a YAML file in tools/structure-diagrams/inputs/, with nodes placed by level and column, edges typed as control, feedback, funding or data, and optional stakeholder views. Edit the YAML and run python3 tools/structure-diagrams/render.py. The renderer places every box on a grid and routes lines at right angles, with each control and feedback pair drawn as two parallel lines, in the manner of Leveson's control structures. It replaced a Graphviz renderer on 2026-10-17 after the grid layout lab measured both; the earlier diagram tool lab compared the off-the-shelf tools.
