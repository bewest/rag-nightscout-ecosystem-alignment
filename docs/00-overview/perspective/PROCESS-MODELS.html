    What we believe, and how it works¶

    Living document, last changed 2026-10-18 at 57a6db95f.

    For the Nightscout Foundation board, the maintainers of the ecosystem projects, and the community. Perspective (part 2 of the overview; how the overview is organised). Living page, written 2026-10-17. Companion to Control structures, drawn, whose conventions, legend and icons apply here. Every node and edge comes from a source cited under the drawing; where a panel is inferred or proposed, it says so.

    Author's interests: the author maintains cgm-remote-monitor, founded a company that hosts Nightscout commercially, and could be a candidate for paid work under the related proposals. The collaboration rules §5 would apply to that company and to the author like anyone else. Operators in the drawings are roles, not named companies.

    How to read these drawings¶

    Every controller in an STPA structure acts on a process model: its belief about the system it controls. Many hazards come from the gap between that model and how the system actually behaves. Each set below draws one structure three times on one layout, so the boxes line up and the lines that appear, disappear or change their label are the finding.

    • Policy questions are drawn as believed (the community's own reading), as it works today and as it is emerging.
    • The ecosystem's own designs are drawn as believed (what clients, operators and people assume), as it works today (cited to code) and as proposed (cited to the decision or proposal; nothing in a proposed panel is built unless it says so).

    Teal dashed lines are authority: where a party's right to act comes from (a law, an exemption, a licence). Purple dash-dot lines are discretion: control another party exercises at its own choice (terms, enforcement, revocation). The other lines and the icons are as in the legend and icon key.

    Policy: believed, actual, emerging¶

    1·authority: where the authority to act comes from¶

    As believed. The community's own reading of its protections: open-source licences, the DMCA medical-device data exemption, interoperability rights (DIGITAL-RIGHTS, overview). In this picture the person's authority runs straight from the law to their own device and data, and no one else's choices sit between them.

    As it works today. The same rights, read precisely, and the discretion they leave standing. The DMCA exemption covers data access only and is a defence renewed every three years; DIY looping "remains in a legal gray area", and the community operates partly on "the practical reality that manufacturers have generally not pursued enforcement" (DIGITAL-RIGHTS, implications). Five discretion edges reach the person or the projects: a manufacturer's firmware and cloud changes, its takedown notices (Abbott, 2019), cloud terms that ban "unauthorized third-party applications" (Dexcom consumer terms §5.11, vendor note §1.2), the FDA's 2019 warning against unauthorised devices (history), and a phone platform's signing licence, 7 days on a free account and revocable (Apple note §1.1). Regulators' requirements reach manufacturers, including premarket cybersecurity for connected devices under FD&C Act §524B, in effect since 2023-03-29 (FDA, cybersecurity, read 2026-10-17).

    As it is emerging. Two changes are under way at once. Newer devices authenticate the app as well as the person. The community's Omnipod 5 driver presents a controller certificate and signs pod commands (OmnipodKit 928bb79, read 2026-10-17; vendor note §1.6); where that certificate comes from is not described here, and whether the driver runs against a real pod was not checked. The app check is already on two CGM lines: Dexcom G7 and Libre 3 ask for a client credential after the person's code or NFC scan, and the open implementations carry a fixed one that the manufacturer did not issue to them (xdripswift c268542, Juggluco 75399d7, LibreLoop 150d2b1, read 2026-10-17). Tandem pairs with a code shown on the pump, and no app credential was found (pumpX2 6fa9459). Why device-level controls tighten and do not loosen is not settled by any source read. One reading, for counsel and regulatory review: §524B and the FDA's premarket cybersecurity guidance ask manufacturers to show how device interfaces authenticate those that connect, which leaves a manufacturer little reason to keep an unauthenticated path (vendor note §7). And in the EU, the Data Act (applying since 2025-09-12) turns part of the data right from a defence into a duty: the manufacturer must share device data at the person's request, real-time where feasible, including with a third party the person designates (vendor note §3.1). The drawing combines what exists on some devices and in one jurisdiction; it is not a forecast for any one device or market.

    Interpretation. Properties of the emerging landscape, read from the three drawings:

    1. Where a device checks the app, access is bound to identity. A credential is issued to a known party and can be renewed or withdrawn; knowing the protocol is no longer enough.
    2. Authority is delegated, and its withdrawal is the issuer's. Whether revocation follows a published process or the issuer's discretion is decided by the agreement (partner readiness §4).
    3. Rights move from defences to duties, for data first. The Data Act obliges sharing data; no law found obliges a manufacturer to accept commands from another party's software.
    4. The designated third party has to be an entity that can meet the requirements: privacy, security, and the terms the manufacturer's credentials carry (what each activity brings §5).
    5. Requirements flow down by contract. Cybersecurity and quality duties sit with the manufacturer, and reach anyone holding its credentials through the agreement.
    6. Authorisation becomes the binding constraint. The engineering gets cheaper for everyone; who is authorised to use it does not. This is a reading of the direction. Where the person holds the pairing secret (Tandem, Dana, Medtrum, Dexcom G6), engineering is still the constraint, and it has been met. Where the device checks the app (Dexcom G7, Libre 3, MiniMed 780G, Omnipod 5), open access today rests on credentials the manufacturer did not issue to the projects, which new sensors or firmware could stop accepting.

    The characterisations of law here are summaries for discussion, drafts for counsel to review, and not legal advice.

    0·diy-status: under what status a DIY build runs¶

    Draft for review by regulatory counsel. This section summarises published sources for discussion; it is not legal or regulatory advice, and it does not say what any regulator will or will not do.

    People who build and run open-source automated insulin delivery (AID) software for themselves act under a regulatory status that the community describes one way and regulators have described only in part. The drawings keep four positions apart, because that is where the status can change: a person building for themselves, a project publishing source code, anyone handing out built apps, and anyone running the software for someone else.

    As believed. The projects' own pages say the software is not approved and that the person who builds it carries the risk: "not approved for therapy by any government organization" and "You take full responsibility for building and running this system and do so at your own risk" (LoopDocs, read 2026-10-17); "not CE or FDA approved for therapy" (Trio README). The AndroidAPS FAQ gives the community's fullest reading: under EU rules a system of class IIa or IIb is a medical device needing a CE mark, distribution "even giving away for free" is covered, and "Building a medical device for yourself is the only way to use the app within these regulations" (AAPS FAQ, read 2026-10-17). The repository's suggested disclaimer says the software "is NOT a medical device" (DIGITAL-RIGHTS, disclaimers). In this picture regulation reaches manufacturers and people who hand out built apps, and stops short of the person and of published source.

    As regulators have spoken. On 2019-05-17 the FDA warned patients and clinicians against "unapproved or unauthorized devices", noting that "patients may choose to create these systems", and said it is "aware of manufacturers illegally marketing unauthorized devices" (FDA, read 2026-10-17; history). No FDA statement was found on an individual building such software for their own use. US device duties attach to establishments and distribution: registration (21 CFR 807), premarket notification "before ... the introduction or delivery for introduction into interstate commerce for commercial distribution" (807.81(a)), where commercial distribution means a device "held or offered for sale" (807.3(b)); the registration exemptions in 807.65 list no class for a person making a device for their own use (eCFR 807, read 2026-10-17). Separately, 21 U.S.C. 331(a) prohibits introducing an adulterated or misbranded device into interstate commerce without a sale condition (LII, read 2026-10-17). Whether publishing source code, or handing out a built app free, is either of these was not answered by any source read. The regulated route exists: the iCGM, ACE pump and iAGC special controls (cloud layer §2.2), and Tidepool Loop, cleared by 510(k) K203689 on 2023-01-23 as an iAGC, beside the continuing DIY project (precedents). In the EU, the MDR's in-house exemption (Article 5(5)) is for health institutions: "an organisation the primary purpose of which is the care or treatment of patients", and a device used "outside the health institution's legal entity ... cannot be in-house" (MDCG 2023-1, January 2023, not legally binding, read 2026-10-17). No MDR provision for an individual's own build was found. No MHRA statement on DIY AID was found (search, 2026-10-17).

    As it is emerging. Three developments reach the positions differently. FD&C Act §524B (premarket cybersecurity, in effect 2023-03-29; guidance issued 2025-06-27) applies to "a person who submits an application or submission" for a cyber device (FDA, cybersecurity; LII 21 U.S.C. 360n-2, both read 2026-10-17), so it reaches the regulated route, not the person. Apple, from 2026-03-26, requires health and medical apps in the US, EEA and UK to declare whether they are a regulated medical device; a "Yes" needs an EU SRN or FDA owner/operator number, and existing apps must declare "by early 2027" (Apple note §1.7). And the ACE and iAGC decision summaries record interface plans covering contracts and "who is responsible for investigating and reporting complaints", which is a route for an operator under contract with a manufacturer; no manufacturer was found to publish a route for a third party (vendor note §2). The regulated form of running it for others is drawn in 2·controller-operator.

    Interpretation. Properties the three panels show:

    1. The status turns on distribution, not on the code. Every published duty found attaches to placing on the market, commercial distribution or a premarket submission. None found attaches to a person building for their own use; none found settles whether publishing source does.
    2. The person's own build is the least-addressed position. The FDA's only line to it is a warning; the EU's only exemption found is for health institutions. Neither is a permission.
    3. The community's beliefs differ by project. One project reads EU rules as reaching free distribution of built apps; others say only "not approved". The beliefs agree that the person carries the risk.
    4. Running it for others has a regulated form and no published DIY form. The regulated form is a submission and, for interoperable devices, a contract that assigns complaint duties.
    5. New requirements reach the distributor first. §524B and app-store declarations bind whoever submits or publishes; the self-builder's position is the same in the actual and emerging panels.

    Not drawn: the EU AI Act, because whether it reaches a rule-based controller was not determined; the UK's own device regulations, which were not read. The characterisations of law here are drafts for regulatory counsel, not advice; no enforcement intent is inferred from any source.

    Points for a regulatory professional to verify¶

    1. Whether publishing AID source code (no binaries, no sale) is "introduction or delivery for introduction into interstate commerce" of a device under 21 U.S.C. 331(a) or 21 CFR 807.
    2. Whether free distribution of a built app is "commercial distribution" (807.3(b): "held or offered for sale") or otherwise triggers registration, listing and premarket notification.
    3. Whether an individual building for personal use is a "manufacturer" under 21 CFR 807.3(d) or 820.1, given that 807.65 lists no exemption for that case.
    4. MDR Article 2 definitions of "manufacturer", "placing on the market" and "making available on the market", read from EUR-Lex (not reachable on 2026-10-17); whether "commercial activity" covers free supply by a hobbyist or project, as the AAPS FAQ reads it.
    5. Whether any MDR provision or national rule addresses a patient's own build; MDCG 2023-1 covers only health institutions.
    6. The MHRA's position, if any, and the UK Medical Devices Regulations 2002 on own-use builds.
    7. Whether the FDA's 2019-05-17 notice remains current, and whether any later FDA statement on DIY or open-source AID exists.
    8. Whether a person or entity running AID software for others (including paid builders or hosted controllers) is a manufacturer, and whether 807.65(i) (service through "a previously manufactured device") could ever apply.
    9. Whether §524B's "cyber device" definition could reach an unsubmitted DIY build (the statute attaches it to a submitter).
    10. Whether answering "No" to Apple's regulated-device declaration is accurate for a DIY AID app, and what that answer implies in each market.

    1·data-rights: getting data out, and letting others in¶

    As believed. The data rights primer names five rights: access, export, share, delegate and audit. Sharing is to be "on your terms, with the scope you choose" and "revocable when circumstances change" (primer; DIGITAL-RIGHTS, overview). In this picture data runs from the vendor cloud to the person's own site, and the person decides who sees it.

    As it works today. US rights are a copyright exemption (a defence, not a duty to provide access), HIPAA (the US health-privacy law) access to what a clinic holds, and state privacy laws (vendor note §5.2). A manufacturer's consumer terms can forbid automated access and "unauthorized third-party applications" (Dexcom §5.11, vendor note §1.2). The ecosystem's real-time data comes from follower services and device protocols the vendors do not document for this use (§1.9). The site's connector is configured with the person's vendor username and password (nightscout-connect README, read 2026-10-17). The vendor's own routes go elsewhere: MiniMed shares with "up to five care partners" and uploads to the clinic (§1.4). On the site, read access is one setting. As documented, the default role readable (*:*:read) lets anyone with the address view everything unless the owner sets denied (cgm-remote-monitor 15.0.8 README, AUTH_DEFAULT_ROLES). Beyond that, sharing means handing over the API secret or a token. There are no scoped, expiring grants (configuration and delegation §5.3).

    As it is emerging. The EU Data Act has applied since 2025-09-12. It gives the user access to product data "continuously and in real-time" where feasible (Art. 4). It also lets "a user, or a party acting on behalf of a user" have the same data sent to a third party (Art. 5), free of charge to the user. The third party may use it only for the agreed purposes (Art. 6) (vendor note §3.1). The European Health Data Space adds proxy services, so that a person can authorise someone else to reach the records clinics hold (Art. 4(2), §3.2). Scoped, revocable delegation for hubs exists only as a design: grants with scopes, constraints and expiry (authority model, "not implemented"). The drawing combines one jurisdiction's law with an unbuilt design; it is not a forecast for any market.

    Interpretation.

    1. The route out of the vendor cloud is the person's own login. Today's real-time path depends on the vendor tolerating it, not on a duty to provide it.
    2. Delegation is shared credentials. Revoking one helper means changing what everyone holds.
    3. The law that creates a duty is European, and covers data only. It obliges sharing on request with a designated party. No law found obliges accepting commands.
    4. Two kinds of proxy are emerging. EHDS proxy covers clinic records, and a Data Act third party covers device data. Neither yet reaches a self-hosted hub's own followers.

    The characterisations of law here are summaries for discussion, drafts for counsel to review, and not legal advice. Points to check: whether CGM values are in the Data Act's scope (Recital 15); the Art. 4(2) health-and-safety carve-out; whether a US non-profit can be a designated third party; and the current HIPAA rule for directing records to a third party (45 CFR 164.524) (vendor note §7).

    1·access-paths: who holds the key on each path¶

    As believed. Every path is open to software that knows the protocol, and the data is the person's to share (DIGITAL-RIGHTS, overview; primer).

    As it works today. Pump paths are mostly open because the secret they need is one the person holds: a code shown on the pump, a serial, or the device itself (device-layer census; DanaKit e157a86, MedtrumKit ef866fa, pumpX2 6fa9459). The CGM paths split. Dexcom G6 needs only the transmitter's ID (CGMBLEKit ea224e7). Dexcom G7 and Libre 3 check the app as well, and the open implementations present a fixed client credential the manufacturer did not issue to them (xdripswift c268542, Juggluco 75399d7, LibreLoop 150d2b1, read 2026-10-17). Two pumps check the app too. The MiniMed 780G's open connectors authenticate as the manufacturer's own mobile app, using that app's key database, after the person starts pairing from the pump (OpenMinimed Documentation 73a7811); both connectors say they are proofs of concept, not for therapy. The open Omnipod 5 driver presents a controller certificate whose source is not described here (OmnipodKit 928bb79; vendor note §1.6). Eversense 365 takes a different route: the open driver signs in to the manufacturer's service with the person's own account to get the device's certificate, so the manufacturer's service is in the path though no check of the app was found (EversenseKit e1bb6fa; its predecessor, the E3, has no link security). The cloud path runs on the person's own login and the vendor's tolerance, and breaks when the cloud changes (evidence §5).

    As it is emerging. Two closures are possible on the device side: an app check that stops accepting the credentials open software presents, and readings sealed end to end for the manufacturer's cloud. The second was not looked for in the census (vendor note §5.1). On the cloud side the EU Data Act adds a duty to share, and the US has none (vendor note §3.1).

    Interpretation.

    1. The key, not the encryption, decides access. Every device on step A uses authenticated or encrypted links, and open implementations work with all of them.
    2. Each step up moves the key towards the manufacturer. On step B the open app depends on the manufacturer's app staying installed; on C, on the manufacturer's service answering the person's sign-in; on D, on the device continuing to accept the credential the open app presents; on E no app on the phone can read the data at all.
    3. Step D is in use today, not only emerging. Every device on it works with open software only through credentials the manufacturer did not issue to the projects: fixed ones carried in the open code (Dexcom G7, Libre 3, MiniMed 780G), and for Omnipod 5 a controller certificate whose source is not described here. A sensor, pump or firmware change that stops accepting them would close the path for every open implementation at once, and whether carrying such a credential is lawful is a question for counsel (vendor note §7).
    4. Loss on the device side and on the cloud side reach different people. The device side stops automation for the person; the cloud side stops remote following for caregivers (partner readiness §1).

    The drawing and ladder are at mechanism level only. The ladder's data and generator are tools/structure-diagrams/access/devices.yaml and access_charts.py.

    1·hosting: who answers for a site run for someone else¶

    As believed. Nightscout is "software you run yourself", written for "the person who runs a Nightscout site: for themselves, for their child, or for someone they care for" (operator upgrade path §1). The licence is AGPL-3.0 and the code comes "WITHOUT ANY WARRANTY" (cgm-remote-monitor README.md, licence section). Here the person who runs the site is the person who relies on it.

    As it works today. Many sites are run by a hosting operator for a subscription. The shared documentation lists nine providers, added by pull request, with no published criteria and no rule for the order. Its introduction reads "If you want to save time and avoid the need to maintain a DIY solution, you have many providers", so the listing presents upgrades and upkeep as the operator's job (GOVERNANCE-TODAY §4). What each operator promises on availability, security and exit is in its own terms, which were not read for this drawing. Health-data law is already in force: the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (16 CFR 318, amended 2024) sets breach notice for health apps outside HIPAA (the US medical-privacy law), and Washington's My Health My Data Act reaches "any legal entity", non-profits included (vendor note §3.5–3.6). Whether either reaches a given operator is a question for counsel.

    As it is emerging. The duties follow the activity, whoever does it. "Hosting or processing anyone's health data" brings a privacy notice, retention rules and breach response; "operating a service that people's data or therapy depends on" brings availability, security and incident duties and a continuity plan (what each activity brings §3). Three changes are proposed, not adopted. The collaboration model's rule 3 lists every provider that meets published criteria and labels any run by the project's own maintainers or founders (COLLABORATION-MODEL §5). Commercial offerings would show they track the shared contract with the same public conformance suite, CNCF's model (ECOSYSTEM-PROGRAMME §2). And the multitenancy design takes bulk hosting as its target, with one operator running many tenants while "self-hosted single-tenant stays first-class permanently" (D1, with per-tenant credentials in D13; execution plan §1); adopted, not released.

    Interpretation. Properties the three panels show:

    1. The licence disclaims the software's warranty; it does not disclaim the operator's duties. "As is" covers the code. The duties of hosting and operating attach to whoever does them.
    2. The person who relies on a site and the person who runs it come apart. The believed picture has one box doing both jobs. Today the operator runs the site and the person pays for it.
    3. The listing is the ecosystem's only lever over hosting, and it has no criteria. Today it adds providers case by case. The emerging picture makes listing conditional on published criteria and public tests, without the foundation taking on an operator's own duties (ECOSYSTEM-PROGRAMME §2b: sponsor by default, operate only by a recorded decision).
    4. Multitenancy concentrates the duty. One operator answers for every tenant at once.

    Caveat. The characterisations of law here are drafts for counsel, not legal advice. Points to check: whether the FTC rule and state health-data laws reach a hosting operator, and which state laws beyond Washington apply; whether listing providers creates endorsement or liability exposure (COLLABORATION-MODEL §8).

    The ecosystem's designs: believed, actual, proposed¶

    3·identity: who is who on a hub, and who may do what¶

    As believed (inferred). The README describes API_SECRET only as "a secret passphrase that must be at least 12 characters long". Client apps ask for it to upload, and people share it with apps and caregivers (authority model, "The Credential Dilemma"). Read this way, the secret is an upload password.

    As it works today (read-derived, cgm-remote-monitor 15.0.8; the lines are unchanged on dev). A request with no credential gets the default roles (index.js:161-169), readable unless the operator changes it (settings.js:39). A matching API secret grants *, every permission (index.js:173-180), including managing subjects and roles (endpoints.js:38-98). An access token resolves to its subject's roles plus the default roles (index.js:222-238). A token can be exchanged for a JWT that lasts 8 hours (enclave.js:58-59). Each subject's token is derived from the API secret, so changing the secret replaces every token (storage.js:187-193). The seven default roles are at storage.js:140-148. This is the access model as the code and the README document it. It is not a security assessment of any release, and it does not show how well each part of the server enforces it.

    As proposed (hosted, TENANCY_MODE=multi; self-hosted single-tenant keeps API_SECRET, D1). The decisions are in the execution plan §1, §2.8 and §2.9: - D13: there is no deployment-wide secret. Each tenant holds its own root credential, which the hosting operator mints through ns-admin. That port is unreachable from outside (D7), and the owner can rotate the credential. - D14: a per-tenant signing key. - D16: three listeners. ns-tenant-admin is a second socket inside ns-api, decided 2026-10-16. - D17: devices keep a native credential, "never Ory". Ory Kratos for people is adopted in principle and gated. Ory Hydra, for delegated access by third-party apps, is deferred.

    A controller registration is bound to the subject that created it, "never the token". The owner may retire, block, release or transfer it (hub-sync design §3.5). Bindings made with the API secret move to the tenant root credential (walk-through 2026-10-16, row 12, commit 66403e0f). None of the proposed boxes is built.

    Interpretation.

    1. One secret is both a key and the root of trust today. It grants every permission and every token derives from it.
    2. The proposal separates the hoster, the owner and the data path into separate credentials and separate sockets.
    3. Identity moves from a shared secret to a subject. Registrations and tokens attach to who created them.
    4. Delegation to people or agents is still undecided. Hydra is deferred, and the grant model is a design.

    1·federation: one sign-in across regional zones¶

    Identity today belongs to each site, and each hosting operator has its own sign-in. The programme proposes a trust federation instead: each party keeps its own identity provider, the foundation defines what counts as recognised participation, and health data stays in the zone whose law applies to it. Zero trust means no request is trusted because of where it comes from: every call carries a credential that is checked, scoped to what it may do, and short-lived.

    As believed (inferred: no survey exists). Each site is its own island, kept safe by a private secret; sharing means giving the secret to apps and people one trusts. The setup reads this way: one API_SECRET per site, and Nightscout deliberately holds no usernames or passwords of its own (ADR-003).

    As it works today. "Identity today is per site": API_SECRET and tokens are issued by each site; in the multitenant target each tenant holds its own root credential and signing key (D13, D14), and devices keep a native per-tenant credential permanently (D17) (programme §5; and 3·identity above). Each hosting operator runs its own sign-in, and the providers are listed with no published criteria (governance §4). One server, Nocturne, registers apps with OAuth dynamic client registration (RFC 7591) against a bundled list of known apps. The programme records "no work item and no decision on the federation".

    As proposed. The trust federation of programme §5: each party keeps its own identity provider and presents verifiable claims; the receiving service decides whether they meet its policy; the foundation "would define what counts as recognised participation. It would not hold anyone's passwords." The standards exist: OpenID Federation 1.0 (final 2026-02-17) supplies trust anchors and signed entity statements, and OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange (RFC 8693) supplies scoped, downstream and delegated tokens. The federation would carry identities, keys, schemas, consent references and audit identifiers as a shared control plane, while health data stays in regional stores under their own rules. The council of members is the governance form of the 2026-01-31 draft community identity-provider proposal (Option A, a federated council), drawn here as roles: a zone operator may be a hosting company, or a national or informal group, recognised on the same published criteria as every other member. None of this is built or decided.

    Two precedents show the governance at scale: - TEFCA, the US Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement: separately operated health information networks, each designated after an application and onboarding, sign one Common Agreement that sets baseline legal and technical requirements and a governing approach; a Recognized Coordinating Entity, the Sequoia Project, coordinates under contract with the federal government (healthit.gov, read 2026-10-17). "Individual access services" is one of its exchange purposes. - eduGAIN, the interfederation of national research and education identity federations: each federation keeps its own members, signed metadata is aggregated and exchanged, and a steering group with one representative per federation approves policy, new members and suspensions (Wikipedia, read 2026-10-17; edugain.org refused the request, so re-check there before quoting).

    Interpretation. Properties the three panels show:

    1. The foundation's role is recognition, not operation. It publishes the criteria and signs statements about members; it holds no passwords and runs no zone (the §2b sponsor-or-operate split applied to identity).
    2. The same criteria for every member. A hosting company and a national or informal group meet one published standard, the "same requirements for anyone" of what each activity brings §5.
    3. Identity is shared; data is not. The control plane crosses zones; health data stays where its law applies.
    4. Zero trust replaces "the secret is private". Each request carries a scoped, short-lived credential, so a helper or agent can be given exactly what it needs and lose it alone.
    5. Governance is visible. A council approves policy, members and suspensions, so recognition and its withdrawal follow a published process rather than one party's discretion.

    Caveat. The federation is a proposal; whether the foundation can act as a trust anchor, and what liability recognition carries, are questions for counsel.

    3·registration: who writes to the hub, and where a command goes¶

    As believed (inferred: the assumptions the next panel tests). The hub knows which controller wrote a record, an agent acts as an agent, and a command that was sent was applied.

    As the code works today (cgm-remote-monitor a8888f0d, read-derived). There is no registry (no registration code under lib/). Readers re-derive the controller from the document's shape, by whether a devicestatus has a loop or an openaps key (lib/plugins/loop.js:50, lib/plugins/openaps.js:82, lib/plugins/iob.js:100-101), so Trio and AndroidAPS, which both write openaps, look the same, and the free-text device string is unreliable (control-plane reconciliation §3.2–§3.3). Access is a shared secret or tokens with default roles; the Loop relay permission is held only by admin (lib/authorization/storage.js:140-148, lib/api2/notifications-v2.js:19). Only one development-only AndroidAPS path records a command and returns an acknowledgement with an id; elsewhere success shows as the resulting record and failure as a free-text Note (technical loops §3.3–§3.4, landscape §6). An automated agent holds a credential someone shared and is indistinguishable from them; delegation grants are "not implemented" (authority model).

    As decided (D18–D20): specified, not built. D18: well-known declarations ship with the hub for today's writers, and controllers own typed registrations; the hub is responsible for observability, the controller for sending accurately and reconciling, and the hub records composition results rather than computing them (plan §2.6.1). A registration is bound to the subject that created it, not to a token; the owner may block, retire, release or transfer it, never edit it (hub-sync §3.5). D19 separates the definition (what data means, identified by its content hash) from the instance (one connected copy with a lease and device or simulated provenance per role). Each role has one source of record, an overlap is flagged, and a command goes only to the live device command sink, or is refused no-command-destination; that role needs the product's binding or the owner's transfer (plan §2.6.2, hub-sync §3.6). D20 scopes quirks to the versions that produce them and splits the client-assigned identifier from the hub-assigned _id (hub-sync §3.7). An agent would register its own instance; role types are open "so roles such as proposing versus acting can be added for agents" (D19 rule 5). Open: the command API, proposed in the commands specification and not decided, the contract per tenancy mode (SPEC-REG-MODES), the first hub (HUB-REG-CRM), the proposals to Nocturne and the controllers, all not started (queue); and how an agent is scoped, limited and revoked, which no decision covers.

    Interpretation. Properties the three panels show:

    1. Identity moves from inference to declaration, and the declaration is bound to a subject.
    2. The hub routes and records; it never doses. One source of record per role and one command destination decide where data and commands go, not what the controller does with them.
    3. Requested and applied become separate records, though the command record is not yet specified.
    4. The owner gains a control the code lacks: acting on one registration without rotating a secret everyone shares.
    5. An agent stops being a borrowed identity once it registers; its scope is still open.

    Caveat. Read from code and design pages, not run; every proposed box is implemented in no hub.

    2·data-commons: how Nightscout data reaches research¶

    As believed. What each party says it needs: people want "control of their own data" and offer "consent to research"; researchers want "consented, well-described data without taking custody of it" (COLLABORATION-MODEL §2).

    As it works today. Data reaches research by two routes. In the first, a person hands over an export or a readable token (a credential that lets a program read their site), and a researcher converts it one site at a time: tools/ns2parquet/ns_fetch.py reads a site address and token from an environment file (lines 36–81; read from code). This repository's schema corpus is 11 consenting sites, with every recorded value passed through one redaction step (typed-schema evidence §1.3). tools/nsprobe reverses the direction: a data holder runs the probes on their own machine and only counts leave it, with at least three sites behind any named value (nsprobe README; MIN_SITES = 3 at tools/nsprobe/privacy.py:34). In the second route, the foundation's Nightscout Data Transfer (2017) moves a site's data to Open Humans, a research-data platform, and on to data commons there (history). The OpenAPS Data Commons page says members "may remove your data … at any time", that data already given to researchers "may have already been shared", and that raw data is accessed by the volunteer administrators and the projects they authorise (Open Humans, read 2026-10-17). Ethics (IRB) review, consent and withdrawal terms, and data-use agreements are a duty of research with identifiable data (what each activity brings §3); no shared record of such review was found in the sources read.

    As proposed. The datalake proposal asks the board for a 90-day pilot (ECOSYSTEM-PROGRAMME §6). Participants opt in through foundation-approved consent with a revocation and deletion path. Hosting operators may offer that opt-in at sign-up. A contracted implementation team runs intake and normalisation. A foundation sponsor and advisory reviewers decide consent, access and publication, and the foundation "remains accountable" (proposal, board draft, "operating flow" and "consent model"; bewest/ns-data-proposal at 18df3a0). Its roadmap lists federated analysis as a later expansion candidate, not a pilot output. That follows OHDSI's pattern: "data remains at the site … Only aggregate results are shared" (PRECEDENTS; ECOSYSTEM-PROGRAMME §5, "where data may go"). The proposal names legal, privacy and methods review, not IRB review.

    Interpretation. Properties the three panels show:

    1. Consent today is per route, not per person. No one record shows a person where their data went.
    2. Withdrawal stops future use, not copies already shared. This is true of today's commons. The proposal adds a deletion path; it does not say what happens to extracts already delivered.
    3. Two directions compete: pooling, or analysis where the data is. The pilot pools consented exports in one governed store. nsprobe and the OHDSI pattern keep data with its holder and move only aggregates.
    4. The proposal separates sponsor from operator, the §2b split applied to research, and puts consent collection with hosting operators, which joins this drawing to 1·hosting.

    Caveat. The characterisations of research ethics and privacy here are drafts for an ethics or privacy professional and for counsel, not advice. Points to check: whether the pilot needs IRB review, and under which institution; and what consent, de-identification and publication review an extract needs in each region (ECOSYSTEM-PROGRAMME §9, point 8).

    2·shared-infra: shared operations, and who runs them¶

    As believed (inferred): someone is doing each shared operation.

    As measured today. Of 20 repositories (2026-10-02), none has a SECURITY policy of its own and 4 have private vulnerability reporting on; reports arrive in Discord, Facebook, Zulip, Gitter and GitHub; the provider listing changes by pull request with no published criteria; the foundation's resource-request page names no review criteria; no cross-project body is named (governance §2–§6). No named security contact, complaint workflow, SBOM or build-provenance record was found (partner readiness §3). The conformance suite lives in this repository: 85 vector files, an oref0 runner, and AndroidAPS and Loop runners not implemented (conformance/; tools/conformance_suite.py:43-55). This repository's CI runs Python syntax and spec validation; the suite and the queue gates run by make, outside CI. The 15.0.9 run records are the nearest thing to release evidence (quality system §4.1).

    As proposed. Four shared operations, "none exists yet as shared work" (programme §2c); two are drawn, with the conformance kit, registry and documentation. The foundation sponsors by default and operates only by a recorded decision naming the operator, its contract, insurance and successor (§2b). One intake takes three streams and refers reportable events to the responsible manufacturer; intake is not legal responsibility (partner readiness §6). Security contacts and private reporting per project "can start at once" (§8). The registry, the credential authority and hosting are kept apart (§7); the foundation would define recognised participation (programme §5). The quality kit gives one run record and a suite any server can run; each project decides whether to join (quality system §4–§5). No change to documentation is proposed.

    Interpretation. Properties the three panels show:

    1. Two operations have no runner today. Registry and intake have no edge in the actual panel.
    2. What exists is engineering, held in one repository; the operational evidence mostly does not.
    3. Sponsoring and operating are different edges: funding to projects, control of a service others depend on to a named operator, by decision.
    4. The intake closes two loops: regression tests back to projects, referrals out to manufacturers.

    Caveat. Counts are as measured on the dates given; re-measure before quoting. Whether operating an intake creates duties for the foundation, and how referrals meet a manufacturer's reporting duties (21 CFR Part 803), are drafts for counsel and a regulatory professional, not advice.
