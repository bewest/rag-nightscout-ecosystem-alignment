    Vendor programmes and data rights: what is open, to whom, and under what law¶

    Living document, last changed 2026-10-17 at b2465f140.

    For the Nightscout Foundation board, the maintainers of the ecosystem projects, and the community. Research note, 2026-10-17. Every external fact below carries its source and was read on 2026-10-17 unless it says otherwise. Facts carried over from the cloud-layer census were read on 2026-09-30 and say so. Anything that could not be checked against a primary source is marked not verified.

    Draft for legal review. This note is not legal advice. Wherever it describes what a statute, regulation, contract or terms of use says or how it might apply (§3, §4 and the tables in §5), it is a non-lawyer's reading for review by the foundation's counsel, and the FDA items also by a regulatory professional, before anyone relies on them. The specific points for a reviewer are listed in §7.

    It adds to DIGITAL-RIGHTS (rights that do not depend on a manufacturer's permission), the data rights primer (the five rights: access, export, share, delegate, audit), PARTNER-READINESS (tiers A, B and C of access; what an agreement must carry) and the cloud-layer census §2 (documented routes out of vendor clouds, and the FDA and data-law texts read on 2026-09-30). It does not repeat them. Apple's developer tiers are covered in the sibling note apple-accountability; device-protocol facts come from the device-layer census, at mechanism level only.

    Terms used¶

    term meaning here
    API application programming interface: a documented way for one program to ask another for data
    OAuth a login pattern in which a person approves an app's access on the vendor's own page, and can later withdraw it, without giving the app their password
    sandbox a test copy of a vendor's API that returns made-up data
    real-time / delayed real-time means each reading is available within minutes of the sensor taking it; delayed means a fixed lag (for example one hour) or upload in batches
    iCGM, ACE pump, iAGC FDA device types for a CGM, an insulin pump and a dosing algorithm that are cleared to work with each other's kind from other manufacturers (21 CFR 862.1355, 880.5730, 862.1356)
    interface specification the technical description of how another device or program may talk to a device
    special controls the conditions FDA attaches to a device type, which every device of that type must meet
    connected product, data holder, user, third party EU Data Act terms: the device; the company that holds its data (often the manufacturer running the cloud); the owner or person using it; and anyone the user asks the data holder to send the data to
    TPM / circumvention technological protection measure (for example encryption or authentication) and getting around it; §1201 of the US copyright law forbids circumvention unless an exemption applies
    trafficking offering a tool to the public whose main purpose is circumvention; §1201(a)(2) forbids it, and the triennial exemptions do not cover it

    1.2 Dexcom¶

    • Developer API. Registered developers get six read-only endpoints (alerts, calibrations, dataRange, devices, egvs, events) for G6, G7, G7 15-day, ONE and ONE+. Data from the phone apps is "available from the Dexcom API with a one-hour delay in the United States", three hours outside; data uploaded from a receiver over USB is available at once. https://developer.dexcom.com/docs/dexcomv3/endpoint-overview/
    • Tiers. Sandbox with four simulated users, not to be used "for formal testing, validation, or training algorithms" (https://developer.dexcom.com/docs/dexcom/sandbox-data/); Limited Access, which an "Individual" may apply for and which allows "up to 5 authorized users"; Full Access, for which commercial developers "must submit a request for a commercial partnership". https://developer.dexcom.com/docs/dexcom/scopes-access/ , https://developer.dexcom.com/docs/dexcom/getting-started/
    • Review and terms. "Dexcom Strategic Partnerships team will review your application"; Dexcom may require technical and interface changes; "you will be sent a standard, non-negotiable Data Licensing Agreement". The agreement's text is not public (not verified: whether it restricts therapeutic or dosing use). There is "currently no way for developers to request a subset of these scopes". Same page.
    • Revocation. "Users can revoke their authorization at any time at Dexcom.com"; Dexcom then stops sending data, but "any information previously provided to the developer's app will not be affected by this revocation". Same page. Revocation of a developer by Dexcom: not described.
    • Real-time partners. FDA cleared the "Dexcom Partner Web APIs" in July 2021 for "invited third-party developers"; Garmin and Teladoc's Livongo were named (https://www.biospace.com/fda-clears-dexcom-real-time-apis-for-third-party-apps-and-devices, a trade report of the 2021-07-15 press release; Dexcom's own release returned 403). Dexcom's developer pages read today do not describe a real-time tier or how to be invited.
    • Partner pages. Dexcom says it integrates with "over 90 connected health apps" and pumps, and links a "Partnership Interest" intake form; no criteria are published. https://www.dexcom.com/partnerships/integrations-and-compatibility
    • Consumer terms (effective 2023-01-26), §5.11: users may not "use any automated process or service (such as a bot, a spider, or periodic caching of information)", "copy or scrape data from any DexCom Product", "reverse engineer or derive the source code", or "enable unauthorized third-party applications to access DexCom Products or DexCom Services". https://www.dexcom.com/en-us/legal/terms-of-use

    1.3 Abbott¶

    • Partner list, not a programme. Abbott's integrations page lists the MiniMed 780G, MiniMed Go and MiniMed Flex (with the Instinct sensor Abbott makes), NovoPen 6 and Echo Plus, mylife Loop (CamAPS FX with YpsoPump), Omnipod 5, a LibreLinkUp–Omnipod 5 cloud connection, Tandem t:slim X2, Beta Bionics iLet, "twiist AID System Powered by Tidepool" and Omada Health. "This list will be updated as other devices, apps, software, and systems are evaluated by Abbott Diabetes Care." It warns that use with devices "that are not listed may cause inaccurate glucose readings". No developer programme, API or SDK is mentioned. https://www.diabetescare.abbott/partnerships/integrations/en.html
    • Data aggregators. One aggregator documents two routes: the person's LibreView login, which "is no longer supported for new connections", and the person sharing their account with the aggregator's LibreView practice (clinic) account, so that it "accesses the patient CGM data as a member of the LibreView practice". https://docs.junction.com/wearables/guides/abbott-libreview (an aggregator's page, not Abbott's; Abbott's own terms for practice accounts were not read).
    • Consumer terms for LibreLink and LibreLinkUp: not read; the search did not return them. The census records that LibreLinkUp terms prompts block connectors until the person accepts them (cloud-layer §1.3, Nocturne 828b25ce).

    1.4 MiniMed (Medtronic Diabetes)¶

    • Corporate change. MiniMed Group, Inc. completed its separation from Medtronic plc on 2026-10-15 (8-K exhibit, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002062583/000110465926116441/tm2627653d1_ex99-1.htm, read through a search summary, not verified in full). Repository pages that say "Medtronic CareLink" now refer to a MiniMed service.
    • Sharing routes. The current support page lists automatic upload to the care team's CareLink Clinic and the CareLink Connect app to "share your MiniMed pump and sensor data along with the system alerts with up to five care partners". It names no third-party app, export file or API. https://www.minimed.com/en-us/support/product-support/minimed-flex/sharing-your-data
    • Interoperability. Medtronic announced on 2025-09-02 that FDA cleared the SmartGuard algorithm as an iAGC so the 780G can use the Instinct sensor, the 780G having been cleared earlier as an ACE pump (https://news.medtronic.com/2025-09-02-FDA-Clears-MiniMed-TM-780G-System-to-Enable-Integration-with-the-Instinct-Sensor,-Made-by-Abbott,-and-Approves-Use-in-Type-2-Diabetes, read through a search summary).
    • Looked for and nothing published found: a developer or partner API page on minimed.com or medtronicdiabetes.com.

    1.5 Tandem¶

    • Tandem Source "is not intended for use as a remote monitoring tool for caregivers" and "Data uploads to the Tandem Source platform do not happen in real time." No partner is named. https://www.tandemdiabetes.com/support-center/pumps-and-supplies/cartridges/article/data-sharing-capability-tandem-source
    • Glooko documents a user-authorised link from Tandem Source (cloud-layer §2.1, 2026-09-30).
    • ACE pump. Tandem's FDA decision summary for the first ACE pump (DEN180058) records a plan covering "their approach to working with connected device companies regarding contractual approaches, interfaces for data communication and exchange, and post-market reporting procedures and responsibilities (e.g., who is responsible for investigating and reporting complaints, malfunctions, and adverse events)". https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN180058.pdf
    • Looked for and nothing published found: a page on tandemdiabetes.com describing how a third party may apply for the interface specification or integrate a controller.

    1.6 Insulet (Omnipod)¶

    • The innovation page says Omnipod 5 "works with leading sensor brands" and names Dexcom and Glooko as trademark licensors; it mentions no third-party algorithm, developer programme or API. https://www.omnipod.com/innovation
    • A 2023 Tidepool reading of Insulet's Omnipod 5 decision summary found "plans covered Insulet's approach to working with third party manufacturers of digitally connected devices" and the 510(k) statement "Future alternate controllers (iAGCs) may be established for use with the Pod" (https://www.tidepool.org/blog/the-inside-scoop-on-third-party-control-for-ace-pumps, 2023-02-01; the Insulet summary itself was not read).
    • Device level: the Omnipod 5 driver in the community kit presents a controller certificate and signs pod commands (OmnipodKit 928bb79, read 2026-10-17). The pod's check is the "credential the manufacturer issues" case in PARTNER-READINESS §1 and §7. Where the driver's certificate comes from is not described in this note.
    • Looked for and nothing published found: a developer, partner or interface-specification page on omnipod.com or insulet.com.

    1.7 Roche, Ypsomed, Sequel, Medtrum¶

    • Roche. Roche Diabetes Care publishes an EMR implementation guide with a "Sandbox API to allow EMRs, PHRs and other systems test certain features" without "full scale onboarding", using mutual-TLS certificates Roche provides (https://simplifier.net/guide/RDCEMRImplementationGuide/sandboxapi). Glooko announced on 2026-03-10 that it will be "the first diabetes data management platform outside of Roche's ecosystem" to integrate the Accu-Chek SmartGuide CGM (search summary of https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260310206611/en/Glooko-to-Expand-EMEA-CGM-Connectivity-with-Roche-Accu-Chek-SmartGuide-Integration). A UK report says newly activated SmartGuide sensors will pair "exclusively with the mySugr app" after a transition (https://www.disn-uk.co.uk/news/roche-diagnostics-accu-chek-smartguide-users-to-transition-to-mysugr-app, search summary, not verified with Roche).
    • Ypsomed. Partnerships with CamDiab (CamAPS FX on YpsoPump) and Dexcom are published as press releases (https://www.ypsomed.com/en/media/details/ypsomed-partners-with-camdiab-ltd-to-drive-on-smartphone-based-adaptive-automated-insulin-delivery-aid.html). A 2018 JDRF-funded project aimed at "open-protocol communication" for the next YpsoPump (https://www.jdrf.org/?p=26795); whether it reached regulatory approval was not verified. Nothing published found for a developer programme or mylife Cloud API.
    • Sequel (twiist). Tidepool says Tidepool Loop is distributed "as a licensed, third-party distributed product, most recently through Sequel Med Tech as twiist Loop" (https://www.tidepool.org/). Diabeloop announced on 2026-01-13 that its DBLG2 algorithm, cleared as an iAGC on 2025-12-18, will be integrated into twiist, with "product readiness by end of 2026 and launch in the US in 2027" (https://www.diabeloop.com/news/company/dbl-sequel-partnership-en). Nothing published found for a developer programme.
    • Medtrum. medtrum.com links consumer apps, a compatible-devices page and a clinician portal; nothing published found for a developer or partner programme (https://www.medtrum.com/; a web search returned nothing relevant).

    1.8 Tidepool¶

    • Platform. Tidepool's API "not marked Internal" is "available to everyone", a client identifier must be requested, and there are separate integration and production environments (cloud-layer §2.1, 2026-09-30). Its public code shows OAuth partner integrations with the Dexcom developer API, Abbott (through a private plugin), twiist and Oura (cloud-layer §1.1).
    • Tidepool Loop. Tidepool describes Tidepool Loop as built on the community Loop app and taken through FDA; its device-partners page names no partner other than twiist and states no licensing terms or partner requirements (https://tidepool.org/loop-device-partners).
    • CMS pledge. "Tidepool Project" is listed among the pledgees in the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Health Tech Ecosystem "Diabetes and Obesity" category, whose pledge is to "connect to CMS Aligned Networks or personal health apps and, with patient consent, securely access relevant health data". No CGM or pump manufacturer appears on that page. https://www.cms.gov/health-tech-ecosystem/early-adopters/diabetes-obesity (page last modified 2026-08-06). The initiative is voluntary (https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/white-house-tech-leaders-commit-create-patient-centric-healthcare-ecosystem).

    1.9 Where the vendor routes leave the ecosystem¶

    Read together with the census: the only published self-service route to CGM data from a manufacturer is Dexcom's, and it is delayed by one to three hours, capped at five people for an individual developer, and gated by a non-negotiable agreement for anything larger. Every real-time route a manufacturer offers is by invitation or bilateral contract. No pump manufacturer publishes a developer route at all. The ecosystem's real-time data still comes from follower services and device protocols that the vendors do not document (cloud-layer §2.1, last paragraph).

    2. Regulatory interoperability (FDA)¶

    The special-controls text of 21 CFR 862.1355 (iCGM), 880.5730 (ACE pump) and 862.1356 (iAGC) is quoted in cloud-layer §2.2. New since then:

    • What the decision summaries say a manufacturer must plan. The first ACE pump (DEN180058) and first iAGC (DEN190034) summaries both record that the sponsor's plan was found adequate "to support and clearly specify expectations, requirements, and interface specifications to potential interoperable devices", and covered contracts, data interfaces and "post-market reporting procedures and responsibilities (e.g., who is responsible for investigating and reporting complaints, malfunctions, and adverse events)" (DEN190034 says "contractual issues"). https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN180058.pdf , https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN190034.pdf. The FDA record therefore expects interface specifications to reach other manufacturers through contracts that allocate complaint handling, which is the shape PARTNER-READINESS §5 and §6 already anticipate.
    • No published route for a third party. No manufacturer page read for §1 describes how a third party may request its interface specification. Tidepool's 2023 reading was that the special controls "do not require that the pump manufacturer provide plans for how their pump can communicate with any controller" (blog cited in §1.6).
    • Cross-maker combinations are now common. FDA clearances and announcements since 2019 have produced pump–sensor–algorithm combinations across manufacturers: Abbott's list in §1.3; the 780G with Instinct; Diabeloop's iAGC on twiist. Each was made by a company contracting with another.
    • FDA statements on third-party interoperability. FDA's 2019 announcement of the first iAGC was titled as "designed to allow more choices for patients"; the fda.gov page returned 404 and is not verified. FDA's guidance "Design Considerations and Pre-market Submission Recommendations for Interoperable Medical Devices" is cited in both decision summaries. No newer FDA statement on interoperability with non-manufacturer software was found.

    3. Data-rights and blocker-removal law¶

    3.1 EU Data Act¶

    Text read from the Official Journal copy at http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32023R2854 (EUR-Lex returned a bot challenge on 2026-09-30, so the Publications Office copy was used).

    • Dates (Art. 50). "It shall apply from 12 September 2025." The Art. 3(1) design obligation applies "to connected products and the services related to them placed on the market after 12 September 2026". Chapter IV (unfair contract terms) applies to contracts concluded after 2025-09-12, and from 2027-09-12 to some older long contracts.
    • The user's own access (Art. 4(1)). Where data cannot be read directly from the product, the data holder must make "readily available data" accessible "without undue delay, of the same quality as is available to the data holder, easily, securely, free of charge, in a comprehensive, structured, commonly used and machine-readable format and, where relevant and technically feasible, continuously and in real-time". A contract term that excludes or varies the user's rights "shall not be binding on the user" (Art. 7(2)). This right does not need a third party: a person could receive their own data into a tool they run themselves (Recital 22 mentions processing "within an information and communications technology (ICT) environment chosen by the user").
    • Sharing with a third party the user chooses (Art. 5(1)). "Upon request by a user, or by a party acting on behalf of a user", the data holder must make the same data available to a third party, "free of charge to the user", "continuously and in real-time" where relevant and feasible. The data holder may charge the third party "reasonable" compensation (Art. 9(1)); where the recipient is an SME (small or medium-sized enterprise) "or a not-for-profit research organisation", it may not exceed the cost of making the data available (Art. 9(4)).
    • Gatekeepers excluded (Art. 5(3), 6(2)(d)). Companies designated as gatekeepers under the Digital Markets Act (Apple, Google and others) may not be the third party, and a third party may not pass the data to one.
    • What a third party must accept (Art. 6). Use only "for the purposes and under the conditions agreed with the user"; erase when no longer needed; no profiling unless needed for the service; no onward sharing except by contract with the user; not use the data "to develop a product that competes with the connected product from which the accessed data originate"; not use it "in a manner that has an adverse impact on the security of the connected product".
    • Carve-outs a manufacturer can use.
    • Security and health (Art. 4(2)). Access or sharing may be contractually restricted "if such processing could undermine security requirements of the connected product, as laid down by Union or national law, resulting in a serious adverse effect on the health, safety or security of natural persons". A refusing data holder must notify the national competent authority.
    • Trade secrets (Art. 4(6)–(8), 5(9)–(11)). Disclosure under confidentiality measures; withholding if measures are not agreed; refusal "in exceptional circumstances" where serious economic damage is "highly likely". Each refusal must be reasoned in writing and notified to the authority, and can be challenged (Art. 4(9), 5(12)).
    • Derived data (Recital 15). Information "inferred or derived ... by means of proprietary, complex algorithms" is outside scope. Whether a CGM's glucose value (computed by the manufacturer's algorithm from the sensor signal) is "pre-processed" (in scope) or "derived" (out) is the single most consequential open question for diabetes (§7).
    • What it does not do. The user and third party "shall not use coercive means or abuse gaps in the technical infrastructure of a data holder which is designed to protect the data in order to obtain access" (Art. 4(11), 5(5)). The Act is "without prejudice to" intellectual-property law, including the EU copyright directive that carries the anti-circumvention rule (Art. 1(8)). It gives a right to ask and a complaint route (Art. 37), not a licence to reverse-engineer.
    • Medical devices. Recital 14 lists "medical and health devices" among connected products, and the Commission FAQ (v1.4, 2026-01-22, still the latest listed on https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-frequently-asked-questions-about-data-act) says the Act "applies to all connected products, including those that are subject to specific type approval or conformity assessment regimes (e.g. ... medical devices)" (cloud-layer §2.2).
    • Pending change. The Commission's "digital omnibus" proposal includes Data Act amendments. Secondary reports say the data part had not reached trilogue (negotiation between Parliament and Council) by late 2026 (not verified; procedure 2025/0360(COD) should be checked).
    • Small manufacturers. Chapter II does not apply to micro and small enterprises that are not linked to larger ones (Art. 7(1)).

    3.2 European Health Data Space¶

    Read from http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32025R0327. People get a right to access "at least" the priority categories (patient summaries, prescriptions, images, results, discharge reports) "processed for the provision of healthcare" through national access services (Art. 3), with proxy services so a person can authorise another person (Art. 4(2)), and portability between healthcare providers (Art. 7). Medical-device manufacturers that claim interoperability with EHR systems must meet the EHR interoperability and logging requirements (Art. 27(1)). Device data reach EHDS mainly as a secondary-use category, "personal electronic health data automatically generated through medical devices" (cloud-layer §2.2). Reading: EHDS strengthens access to what a clinic holds; it does not by itself oblige a CGM or pump cloud to share with a person's chosen tool.

    3.3 US information blocking (21st Century Cures Act)¶

    The actors are health care providers, developers of certified health IT, and health information networks and exchanges; device makers are actors only if they hold certified health IT (cloud-layer §2.2, 45 CFR 171.102). New since 2026-09-30: the HTI-5 proposed rule (published 2025-12-29, comments closed 2026-02-27) would cut certification criteria and change some exceptions; no final rule was found (search summaries of https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/astps-hti-5-relaxes-certification-criteria-while-tightening-information-blocking-exceptions/ and healthit.gov materials, not verified against the Federal Register, whose API did not answer).

    3.4 DMCA §1201 exemptions (37 CFR 201.40)¶

    Current text read from eCFR (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-37/chapter-II/subchapter-A/part-201/section-201.40):

    • (b)(7) medical device data: "Literary works consisting of compilations of data generated by medical devices or by their personal corresponding monitoring systems, where such circumvention is undertaken by or on behalf of a patient for the sole purpose of lawfully accessing data generated by a patient's own medical device or monitoring system. Eligibility for this exemption is not a safe harbor from, or defense to, liability under other applicable laws, including without limitation the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986, or regulations of the Food and Drug Administration." The 2015 limits to implanted devices and passive monitoring are not in the current text.
    • (b)(17) medical device repair: programs in "a lawfully acquired medical device or system ... when circumvention is a necessary step to allow the diagnosis, maintenance, or repair".
    • (b)(9) phones: circumvention on smartphones and "portable all-purpose mobile computing devices" for "interoperability of such applications" or removal of software. It covers the phone, not the medical device.
    • Renewal (10th triennial rulemaking). Notice of inquiry 2026-06-09; renewal petitions due 2026-08-24; comments on them due 2026-09-28; renewed exemptions would run October 2027 to October 2030 (https://www.copyright.gov/1201/2027/). The Coalition of Medical Device Patients and Researchers petitioned to renew (b)(7). The renewal page lists no opposition to (b)(7). It lists oppositions to the repair exemption (b)(17) from AdvaMed (the medical-technology trade association) and Philips, and a comment from the Joint Creators (games, film and music trade bodies) that does not oppose the exemption but addresses one petition (https://www.copyright.gov/1201/2027/petitions/renewal/; AdvaMed and Joint Creators filings read). No proposed rule had been posted on the read date.
    • Unchanged limits. No exemption covers trafficking in circumvention tools (17 U.S.C. §1201(a)(2)), sending commands to a device, or breach of contract or terms of use.

    3.5 FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (16 CFR 318)¶

    From eCFR §318.2 (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318): a "personal health record" is an electronic record "that has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources and that is managed, shared, and controlled by or primarily for the individual"; "health care services or supplies" include "any online service such as a website, mobile application, or internet-connected device that provides mechanisms to track diseases, health conditions"; a breach "includes an unauthorized acquisition ... that occurs as a result of a data breach or an unauthorized disclosure". Vendors of personal health records and related entities must notify people, the FTC and, for 500 or more people, the media (https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/health-breach-notification-rule). Reading: this grants no access right; it is an obligation that a hosted service or a designated third party outside HIPAA would probably carry.

    3.6 State consumer health-data laws¶

    Washington's My Health My Data Act (https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true):

    • "Consumer health data" includes "Bodily functions, vital signs, symptoms, or measurements" of health status (RCW 19.373.010(8)(b)(v)).
    • From 2024-03-31 a consumer may confirm and "access such data, including a list of all third parties and affiliates" it was shared with, withdraw consent, and have it deleted, with deletion passed on to every recipient (RCW 19.373.040).
    • A "regulated entity" is "any legal entity" doing business in or targeting Washington; the definition does not exclude non-profits. HIPAA-protected health information is exempt (RCW 19.373.100). Violations are enforced through the state Consumer Protection Act (RCW 19.373.090).
    • Reading: the access right is to confirmation, a copy and the recipient list; it does not require a machine-readable or real-time feed. It also places consent and deletion duties on any organisation that receives Washington residents' glucose data.
    • Other states (for example Nevada and Connecticut) have related laws; not read.

    3.7 Right to repair¶

    • Minnesota (Stat. 325E.72, in force 2024-07-01): "Nothing in this section applies to manufacturers or distributors of a medical device as defined in the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act" (https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325E.72).
    • New York, Oregon, California, Colorado: described by trade and advocacy sources as excluding or not reaching medical devices; statutes not read. Bills aimed at medical-device repair were pending in Vermont (H.160) and Rhode Island (H 7180) in 2026 (search results; not verified).
    • EU Right to Repair Directive (EU) 2024/1799: its repair obligations reach only the products in Annex II (washing machines, dishwashers, refrigerators, displays, welding equipment, vacuum cleaners, servers, phones and tablets, tumble dryers, light-transport batteries); medical devices are not listed (http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32024L1799).
    • Reading: repair laws address parts and manuals, not data access; where checked, they exclude medical devices. The only repair route for diabetes devices found is the federal §1201 (b)(17) exemption, which industry is opposing in the current cycle.

    3.8 Other jurisdictions and the phone¶

    • EU Digital Markets Act. In March 2025 the Commission specified how Apple must give third-party connected devices access to nine iOS features in the EU, including background execution (all measures "no later than end of 2026"), notifications and proximity pairing (by 2026-06-01) (https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/developer-portal/interoperability/apple-specification-proceedings-interoperability-connected-devices-and-request-based-process_en). These concern the phone's treatment of accessories, which matters to apps that keep a Bluetooth link to a sensor or pump; they do not open the medical device. Apple's tiers and EU alternative distribution are in apple-accountability §1.
    • UK. Part 1 (smart data) of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 was commenced on 2025-08-20 (SI 2025/904, search summary); it enables sector schemes by later regulations. No health or device scheme was found (not verified).

    5.2 The maintainer's working view, tested¶

    The view: technology blockers are increasingly being removed, so the picture of success is increasingly clear regardless of who does it; mechanisms like those that let a person build and run software on their own Apple device are needed to protect data rights in diabetes.

    Supported:

    • Between companies, blockers are coming down quickly. FDA's modular classifications have produced cross-maker pump, sensor and algorithm combinations (§2), and the FDA record expects interface specifications to be shared by contract.
    • In the EU, the Data Act creates, for the first time, a legal duty to give a person and their chosen third party their device data, in real time where feasible, free to the person, and it voids contract terms that cut that right down.
    • In the US, the §1201 data exemption has been renewed every cycle and drew no opposition in the petition phase of this one.

    Not supported, or contradicted:

    • Device-level protections are increasing, not decreasing. Each new generation raises the cost of independent access. Pumps found still pair with a secret the person holds (Tandem, Dana, Medtrum). Dexcom G7 and Libre 3 already check the app, and open implementations work only by carrying a fixed credential the manufacturer did not issue to them (xdripswift c268542, Juggluco 75399d7, LibreLoop 150d2b1, read 2026-10-17). The MiniMed 780G and Omnipod 5 check the app too; the 780G is met by the manufacturer's own mobile-app keys, and Omnipod 5 as in §1.6. For such a device knowledge of the protocol is not enough.
    • The removals run through channels that require being a company with a contract. Every real-time vendor route, every interface specification and every cross-maker integration found is bilateral. No manufacturer publishes a route for a non-profit, an individual developer or an open-source project beyond Dexcom's delayed, five-user tier. "Regardless of who does it" does not hold: today it matters a great deal who does it.
    • The US has no general equivalent of the Data Act. US rights found are a copyright exemption (a defence, not an access duty), HIPAA access against clinics, and state privacy laws without format or timing duties.
    • The Data Act gives a right to ask, not a right to build. It keeps copyright and trade-secret protections, lets a manufacturer restrict sharing where security could cause "a serious adverse effect on the health, safety or security of natural persons", and forbids getting data by abusing technical gaps. For insulin-related devices the health-and-safety carve-out is the one most likely to be tested. It also may not reach the glucose value itself if that counts as derived data.
    • The Apple analogy is partial. A person can build and run software on their own iPhone because Apple chooses to allow it (and in the EU because the DMA requires some openness). No law obliges a pump or CGM maker to offer the equivalent, and no right-to-repair law reaches medical devices.

    Verdict on the view as first worded: the view holds for regulated, company-to-company interoperability and for EU data access. It does not hold for independent access to devices, for control, or for the US: the blockers that stand in the ecosystem's way specifically (device authentication, issued credentials, terms of service, bilateral-only routes, three-yearly copyright exemptions) are not being removed, and some are growing.

    The view as the maintainer clarified it (2026-10-17). "Regardless of who does it" means that the requirements are the same for anyone. In the US anyone may meet them who strives to and fulfils all of them; in practice that means a company is required. A non-profit has no real advantage in meeting them; what it offers is a layer of oversight, at one level of governance, over many for-profits, which answers the community's sociological needs. On the technology side, the cost of the engineering is falling: coding agents are used for increasingly ambitious projects, device connectivity among them. Tested against the same evidence:

    • Same requirements for anyone: supported. The FDA classifications in §2 are open to any manufacturer that meets the special controls; Tidepool, a non-profit, went through the same 510(k) route as a company would (PRECEDENTS). Dexcom offers one "standard, non-negotiable Data Licensing Agreement" (§1.2). The Washington act's definitions do not exclude non-profits (§3.6). Apple asks healthcare apps to come from "a legal entity that provides the services, and not … an individual developer", and its only non-profit concession is a fee waiver for organisations that sell nothing (Apple note §1.3).
    • A legal entity with contracts is required in practice: supported. "A company" is narrower than the evidence. Every real-time route and interface specification found is a contract between legal entities (above); none is open to an individual or an unincorporated project. Tidepool, a non-profit, took the same 510(k) route a company would (PRECEDENTS), so what is required is an entity with a quality system and contracts. In practice that is usually a company.
    • The non-profit's role is oversight, not an advantage in meeting requirements: consistent with the precedents. CNCF certifies vendors' offerings against one upstream with the same public tests, and the Open Home Foundation binds its commercial partners by contract to contribute a majority of their profit from licensed products; neither carries those companies' own regulatory duties (PRECEDENTS). Whether a manufacturer values such a layer is not shown by any source read.
    • Falling engineering cost: plausible, not measured here. No source read measures the effect of coding agents on device-integration work. It also bears on a different blocker from the ones that are growing: agents lower the cost of building an integration, while device authentication, app attestation and issued credentials (§1.6) decide who is authorised to use one. Those are met by the entity and its agreements, not by engineering.

    Read this way, the evidence supports the view: the engineering is becoming cheaper for everyone, the requirements are the same for everyone, and what stands between the ecosystem and the devices is being an entity that meets them. That is the structure the proposed ecosystem drawing (1·proposed, 2·agreement, 2·controller-operator) shows.

    5.3 What an organisation would need to use each right¶

    right or route what the organisation would have to be or have
    Data Act third party (EU) a user-authorisation flow by which the person asks the data holder to send data to it; a GDPR legal basis for health data (likely explicit consent) and probably an EU representative if not established in the EU; written purposes, erasure and no-onward-sharing rules matching Art. 6; trade-secret confidentiality measures on request; acceptance that it may not build a "competing connected product"; capacity to complain to a national authority or go to a certified dispute body when refused; willingness to pay cost-based compensation (whether a US 501(c)(3) counts as an SME or a "not-for-profit research organisation" under Art. 9(4) is for counsel)
    Data Act user access via a self-hosted tool nothing institutional: the person requests their own data. An organisation could publish request templates and track refusals as evidence
    DMCA (b)(7), "on behalf of a patient" acting only for named patients, for data access only; no distribution of circumvention tools (anti-trafficking); separate compliance with CFAA and terms; evidence for each renewal cycle
    Dexcom developer API a registered developer; for more than five users, a commercial partnership, the non-negotiable Data Licensing Agreement and Dexcom's review; the 1–3 h delay
    FDA-regulated interoperability (ACE/iAGC) to be a "connected device company" in a manufacturer's contract: a manufacturer's quality system, complaint and adverse-event handling allocated by contract (§2), and in practice a cleared product
    HIPAA access through clinics the person's request to their clinic; the organisation as a recipient the person designates (the precise rule for designating a third party is not verified here)
    Washington My Health My Data Act as a recipient of Washington residents' data: consent, a consumer health data privacy policy, access and deletion handling
    FTC Health Breach Notification Rule as a probable PHR vendor or related entity: breach detection and notice to people, the FTC and media

    7. Points for a reviewer to verify¶

    1. Data Act scope for CGM data: whether a glucose value computed by the manufacturer's algorithm is "pre-processed" (in scope) or "inferred or derived" (Recital 15, out of scope), and whether a pump's delivery record is product data or related-service data.
    2. Data Act Art. 4(2): whether CGM and pump data sharing could be restricted under the health-and-safety security carve-out, and which "security requirements ... laid down by Union or national law" (for example the Medical Device Regulation) a manufacturer could cite.
    3. Data Act "third party": whether a US non-profit can be a third party and data recipient; whether it qualifies for cost-only compensation under Art. 9(4); whether an EU representative is required; and whether an AID app could be a "product that competes with the connected product" under Art. 6(2)(e).
    4. Data Act Art. 4(11) and 5(5): what "abuse gaps in the technical infrastructure" means for follower-service connectors used with the person's own credentials.
    5. Digital omnibus: current status of the Data Act amendments (procedure 2025/0360(COD)).
    6. 37 CFR 201.40(b)(7): whether "on behalf of a patient" covers an organisation running a hosted service for many patients, and how the anti-trafficking rule applies to publishing source code.
    7. 10th triennial rulemaking: whether any late filing opposes (b)(7); when the proposed rule is due.
    8. FDA: whether the "plans" in the ACE and iAGC decision summaries create any obligation to deal with a qualified third party, or only to have a process; whether any FDA statement since 2019 addresses non-manufacturer software.
    9. Information blocking: whether HTI-5 has been finalised and whether it changes actor definitions.
    10. HIPAA: the current rule for an individual directing a copy of their records to a third party (45 CFR 164.524), after the 2020 court decision that limited parts of it.
    11. Washington act: whether the foundation or a hosted service would be a "regulated entity", and what other state laws (Nevada, Connecticut, New York) add.
    12. Right to repair: the medical-device exclusions in the New York, Oregon, California and Colorado statutes, and the status of the Vermont and Rhode Island bills.
    13. Dexcom terms §5.11: how the bans on automated access and "unauthorized third-party applications" interact with the DMCA (b)(7) exemption and, in the EU, with Data Act Art. 7(2).
    14. §524B as the driver of device changes: whether FD&C Act §524B and FDA's premarket cybersecurity guidance expect a manufacturer to show that a device's interfaces authenticate and authorise those that connect. If they do, that explains why device-level controls tighten and do not loosen, independent of whether §524B reaches a DIY build.
    15. Omnipod 5 credential: where the community driver's controller certificate comes from, and whether that driver runs against a real pod. To be raised with counsel in confidence, not in this public note.
    16. Fixed client credentials: whether carrying, in published source or as a separate download, a client credential that a device checks (Dexcom G7, Libre 3, MiniMed 780G; §5.1) is circumvention or trafficking under 17 U.S.C. §1201(a)(2); whether the (b)(7) data exemption covers its use by or on behalf of a patient; and the same questions under the EU anti-circumvention rule and Data Act Art. 4(11).
