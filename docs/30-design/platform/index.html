    Platform contracts: the index to six layers and eight specifications¶

    Living document, last changed 2026-10-26 at 789446fd4.

    Contributor-facing. Living index, current at main 010e1661 (2026-10-18). Terms are defined in the platform glossary.

    This page maps the work on how controllers (Loop, Trio, AndroidAPS and others) and hubs (cgm-remote-monitor, Nocturne) agree on what records are, who wrote them, and how they move. The aim has three parts:

    • Full audit fidelity. Every stored record says what it is, which copy of which controller wrote it, under which definition, and whether it was requested, applied, mapped or projected.
    • Controller-owned definitions, secured. A controller declares its own typed definitions. The hub accepts them per element, binds them to the credential that wrote them, and never lets one controller redefine another's data.
    • Cost-effective sync. Fewer requests per cycle with typed records, REST for writes and catch-up, a socket for live delivery, and versioned definitions so a new release does not need a full re-sync.

    The material is organised in six layers. A lower layer never depends on a higher one.

    layer question it answers depends on
    L0 Identity Which record, definition, instance or subject is this? —
    L1 Records What typed observations exist, and how are today's documents decomposed into them? L0
    L2 Definitions and instances Who declared what a record means, and which running copy wrote it? L0, L1
    L3 State and intent What was requested, what was applied, what is active now, and why did it end? L1, L2
    L4 Change and sync How are writes matched, re-sent, ordered and delivered over REST and the socket? L0–L3
    L5 Authority Who may register, write, route a command or act for someone else? L0, L2

    Audit fidelity is not a separate layer. Each layer's section below lists what it records for audit.

    Status words used here and in every specification's header. Evidenced: measured or read from source in the corpus or controller code. Decided: the maintainer decided it, recorded in the execution plan. Proposed: written as a rule for the maintainer to decide. Specified: a contract with synthetic conformance cases and a reference decision function in tools/nsschema/, which checks the cases but is not a hub. Implemented: a hub serves it. On 2026-10-18 the L2, L3 and L5 contracts are specified and not implemented; the first hub implementation is HUB-REG-CRM.

    The specification set¶

    Eight specifications and a glossary, one file each under spec/. Each answers one question a reader brings, and each has its own rules, conformance cases and gate.

    file question layer rules status queue
    glossary what does this term mean, and where is it specified? all — written —
    identity which record, definition, instance or subject is this, and when is a write a re-send? L0 I1–I9 written SPEC-ID
    records what typed observations and settings snapshots exist, and how do today's documents decompose into them? L1 R1–R9 written SPEC-RECORDS
    registration how does a controller declare what its data means, and how does a hub accept it and attribute what is written? L2 G1–G5, Q1–Q4 written SPEC-REGISTRATION
    state and intent what was requested, applied and active, who started it, and why did it end? L3 S1–S12 written SPEC-STATE-INTENT
    commands how does a request or a proposal reach a controller, and how does its outcome come back? L3, L5 CM1–CM32 written SPEC-REG-COMMANDS
    plans how is a plan proposed, approved in parts, held by the controller and mirrored by the hub? L3, L5 PN1–PN18 written SPEC-PLANS
    sync-transport how are writes matched, ordered and delivered over REST and the socket, and at what cost? L4 T1–T15 written SPEC-SYNC-TRANSPORT
    authority who may register, write, own, route or act for someone else? L5 AU1–AU6, DR1–DR7 written SPEC-AUTHORITY

    Why these boundaries. Each boundary falls where the reader, the rate of change or the review differs:

    • identity changes rarely and every other specification uses it;
    • records change with the observation catalogue and the corpus;
    • registration changes with hub releases, and is read by both controller and hub authors;
    • state and intent changes with controller releases and their vocabularies;
    • commands carry the safety review of anything that reaches a controller;
    • sync-transport changes with performance work;
    • authority changes with the tenancy and authentication decisions.

    Why not more. The grammar stays in registration: declaring an element and accepting it are read together, and the meaning table is what decides version-conflict. Version-scoped quirks stay there because they are declarations and recorded mappings. The effect and motivation model stays in state and intent while it is a proposal.

    Why not fewer. Commands are kept apart from state and intent, because the request, proposal and acknowledgement lifecycle has its own cases and review, and from authority, which says who may rather than what happens. Authority is kept apart from registration so that binding, the owner's actions and delegation are read in one place.

    Each file should stay readable in a sitting. Registration is the largest; command routing has moved from its §9 to commands §2.

    How they are kept.

    1. One home per rule. Other specifications link to it by section and never restate it. Decisions stay in the execution plan; defects stay in the backfix register.
    2. Stable rule ids. A changed rule keeps its id; a removed id is not reused.
    3. One skeleton. Each file has the same parts, in order:
    4. a header: audience, the commit it is current at, its layer, its decisions and its queue item;
    5. files, rules, the sections, compatibility and open items;
    6. its conformance cases.
    7. Current rules only. History is in git. A section moved out of a dated document leaves its heading, a pointer, and a link to its text at the commit it was taken from.
    8. Generated, gated, one commit. Case and declaration tables are generated (python3 -m tools.nsschema.spec_cases <layer>). node tools/queue/gates/platform-spec.js <layer> checks a specification's cases, links and wording. A rule change lands with its schema and case changes in one commit.

    Who owns each layer¶

    Each layer changes with a different group's work, so each has a natural owner. Status at main 0b0d150f, 2026-10-18:

    layer changes with natural owner engaged today
    L0 identity rarely; every layer uses it hub and controller maintainers together the Loop developers shared an identifier proposal on 2026-10-17, close to D20 rule 5; the reply is drafted, not sent
    L1 records the observation catalogue and the site corpus hub maintainers, with research users specified in this repository
    L2 definitions and instances controller releases each controller team, for its own data proposals to the controllers not started (queue)
    L3 state and intent, commands, plans controller releases; carries the safety review controller teams, with caregiver-app teams specified in this repository; implemented in no hub or controller
    L4 change and sync performance work hub implementers (cgm-remote-monitor, Nocturne) proposal to Nocturne not started
    L5 authority tenancy and authentication decisions hub operators, with the foundation's oversight role decided in part (D7, D13–D19; D17 row 2 open) in the execution plan

    L2 and L3 describe what controllers send and do, and today they are written here, not by the controller teams. They become the controllers' own when those teams review the parts that describe their data and hold a route to change them. Why the cut follows the teams is read in the Conway's Law case study §6.

    Ideas borrowed from Kubernetes¶

    Several parts of this design take what Kubernetes does well for declared, typed resources, and apply it to controllers that share a hub:

    idea in Kubernetes here
    one object shape apiVersion, kind, metadata, spec every registration and definition has the same four members (registration §3)
    declared spec, reported status spec is what is wanted, status what is observed a definition declares what a controller's data means; the owner's listing reports what the hub granted, refused and saw overlap (registration §7–§9); a command record separates the request from its state (commands §4)
    reconciliation a controller drives observed state toward its spec the controller reconciles; each instance renewal re-evaluates every claim, and the hub records what it observes (registration §8–§9)
    leases coordination.k8s.io Lease for liveness and leadership instances hold leases; a lapsed holder loses its roles and is never a command destination (registration §8)
    labels and selectors key-value labels, selected by queries instance labels and read selection (registration §10, §12)
    typed extension resources CustomResourceDefinitions, versioned, with conversion controller-owned definitions with namespaced extensions, version-scoped quirks and projected views for older readers (registration §7, §13, §16)
    admission requests are checked and refused with a reason before they are stored each element is accepted or refused with a reason code, and nothing is rewritten (registration §5)

    What the hub does with these is narrower than a Kubernetes control plane: it schedules nothing and acts on no therapy. It records, routes and refuses; the controller decides and enacts (D18).

    Reading order¶

    For someone new to the series: this page, then the glossary, then intent and workflow landscape §2 and §9 for why the contract exists, then the eight specifications in layer order:

    1. identity (L0): every other specification uses its ids;
    2. records (L1): the typed observations a hub stores;
    3. registration (L2): how a controller declares what those records mean, the largest file;
    4. state and intent (L3): requested, applied and active state;
    5. commands (L3, L5): requests and proposals that reach a controller;
    6. plans (L3, L5): recurring intents approved once and held by the controller;
    7. sync-transport (L4): how all of the above moves;
    8. authority (L5): who may do each of the above.

    Across the eight, control loops is an STPA analysis: it draws the loops the specifications set up, lists each unsafe control action with the rule that prevents it, and names the gaps the specifications now carry as open. Compatibility says, for every app the corpus holds and every layer, what the app does today and what it sees from a hub that implements the specifications without the app changing.

    Two guides walk a controller developer through the specifications step by step, each step naming its rule and the conformance case that shows it: add a setting in your next release and bring a new controller onto a hub. Their hub-side companion, implementing registration in cgm-remote-monitor, tells a hub contributor where each slice goes in the code, in what order, and how it is checked.
