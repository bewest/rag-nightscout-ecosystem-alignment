    Multitenant clinical storage layout: proposal¶

    Record

    Record as of 2026-11-23. A point-in-time document; it is not kept up to date.

    Contributor- and maintainer-facing. Candidate 5 keeps JSONB. Stage 1 is held on the typed-storage branch. Stage 2 was built and parked on 2026-11-24 (devicestatus-hour-chunks); corrections from that build are in section 9. Measured against cgm-remote-monitor 2dee799c8e (the #8898 round core) on PostgreSQL 16.15 and MongoDB 8.0, with 100 synthetic tenants seeded through the product write path at 2 and 14 days of looping history. Figures are labelled measured or read-derived. Raw evidence stays local: HANDOFF-pg-bytes-20261123.md (lane S) and HANDOFF-storage-alternatives-20261123.md (lane R). Decisions already taken live in multitenancy-decisions.md; this page restates none of them.

    1. Summary¶

    The multitenant PostgreSQL layout stores clinical data at about 1.94 MB per tenant-day. The same documents in single-tenant MongoDB 8.0 grow by 0.38 MB per tenant-day with snappy compression and 0.24 MB with zstd. That puts PostgreSQL about 5× larger, at 4.4× MongoDB snappy on the 14-day seed (measured). The difference is the layout, not the data and not the multitenant machinery: - The synthetic documents match the maintainer's real-world range of 0.25–0.6 MB per tenant-day. - Non-clinical tables are about 230 KB per tenant and do not grow with days.

    Candidate 2 (1aaa5af33) includes the removal of the storage_bytes trigger and four redundant indexes. On the matched two-day schema replay, clinical storage fell from 1.865 to 1.546 MB per tenant-day (reproduced). The per-tenant counter preserves restore ordering. The stored evaluator frame serves cold realtime readers. The maintainer kept both. Storage decision and measurements

    Clinical audit and change history. At candidate 2, ordinary inserts and edits write the clinical document. V3 history filters the collection by srvModified (lib/api3/generic/history/operation.js). A soft delete marks the stored document for synchronization. The former size trigger updated the same row a second time. The remaining ordinal trigger maintains one counter per tenant in each ordered collection. Neither path creates database roles or appends an audit copy of each document (read-derived at 1aaa5af33). Five operational audit tables plus owner settings history occupied 557,056 bytes at both two and fourteen days in the 100-tenant census (reproduced, retained pg-bytes-20261123/out/*02-seeded.sizes.tsv). These records cover settings, credentials, policy and lifecycle events. The forecast includes their measured footprint. Removing a hypothetical clinical audit log supplies no further reduction.

    The candidate 5 join took the treatment tie-order correction independently of the typed layout. Reproduced on the held typed branch: default resends and deep ISO lookups regressed against JSONB. A sparse lookup prototype improved the tested queries. Its product qualification and a matched 1k evaluator CPU comparison remain open. Retained evidence HANDOFF-stage1-open-gates-20261124.md.

    The original two-stage proposal priced:

    1. Typed columns for entries and treatments, with the fields that are not typed kept in a small remainder document.
    2. Device status stored as one compressed row per tenant per hour.

    The scratch layouts together imply about 0.36 MB per tenant-day, including an estimated identity-index allowance. The API contract applies to both designs. Each has separate gates in section 6. Device-status implementation decision

    2. The problem, measured¶

    Bytes per stored row (14 days, 100 tenants, written through the product)¶

    collection raw JSON PostgreSQL heap TOAST indexes PostgreSQL total MongoDB snappy, data + indexes
    entries 231 767 0 1,021 1,788 74 + 239
    devicestatus 2,326 1,635 309 948 2,892 576 + 113
    treatments 331 826 0 998 1,824 116 + 331

    Why the rows are large (measured unless marked)¶

    • The original size trigger wrote each row twice. A scratch comparison suggested 27% savings. The later matched replay measured 11.9% for removing the trigger and 17.1% with the four index removals.
    • Indexes are about half of every table. Entries carries 15 indexes and 11 generated columns, copied from single-site MongoDB's index list. Generated columns alone cost 120–136 B per row.
    • Rows under about 2 KB are never compressed. PostgreSQL compresses a value only when the whole row passes about 2,032 bytes. toast_tuple_target and lz4 do not change that entry point: the entries and treatments heaps came out byte-identical with them. Every entries and treatments row, and most device-status rows, are stored uncompressed. WiredTiger compresses every block.
    • jsonb is larger than its text for small documents. A 231-byte SGV is stored as 463 bytes of jsonb, because the stored document also gains _id, srvCreated, srvModified, utcOffset, sysTime and dateString.

    Consequences beyond disk¶

    • Size per tier (measured model, checked to 0.4 % against the 10k restore of 44.24 GB):
    • 10k tenants with 30 days is 586 GB of database;
    • 5k with 30 days is 293 GB.
    • The 10k run's api errors (16.4 % 5xx, against 2.4 % in a same-sized peer run) coincided with autovacuum on a 13 GiB relation running through the load, a buffer hit rate of 0.952, and disk reads 2.7–7× slower than the peer's (measured; the causal chain is read-derived, HANDOFF-pg-sizing-20261123.md). Fewer bytes per row means less to vacuum, cache and read.

    3. What other projects do (read-derived, file:line in lane R's handoff)¶

    project clinical storage what is kept
    Nocturne (PostgreSQL) typed tables, tenant column and row-level security; prediction curves in separate jsonb columns unknown glucose fields and unknown nested openaps/loop keys dropped; about 0.6–0.75 KB per glucose reading and 2.5–3.5 KB per loop status including indexes (estimated from column types)
    nighttroop (SQLite) one STRICT-typed SQLite file per site; raw openaps/loop object as text up to 8 KB unknown fields dropped; predictions deleted after 1 day; devicestatus thinned with age and deleted after 30 days; 20 MB cap per site
    AndroidAPS (Room) glucose and doses typed; devicestatus as JSON text 186 days
    Trio (Core Data) one row per prediction point forecasts 2 days, the rest 90 days
    Loop (Core Data) each dosing decision as one blob 7 days locally
    xDrip / xdripswift glucose typed, no predictions 90–180 days
    single-site Nightscout on MongoDB whole documents, WiredTiger block compression operator tool deletes devicestatus older than N days (default 30)

    Nightscout differs from all of these in one way: it is a hub that returns each uploaded document with its unknown fields, under d8 and d20. So the lessons to take are:

    • compression across records, as WiredTiger and TimescaleDB do;
    • typing only what is queried;
    • not dropping fields;
    • retention, which is a product decision for both modes (section 7).

    4. Proposal¶

    Stage 1 — typed entries and treatments¶

    One row per document. Each query key is stored as a typed column. Every other field is kept in a small rest jsonb holding exactly the fields not represented by a column.

    collection typed columns (proposed) in rest
    entries _id (12-byte ObjectId, or text where id_kind is a string), date bigint, type, sgv int2, mbg int2, direction, device, identifier, srvCreated, srvModified, utcOffset, insertion_ordinal every other field, plus sysTime/dateString when they differ from what date derives
    treatments _id, created_at (text COLLATE "C", as now), eventType, insulin, carbs, duration, rate, absolute, enteredBy, identifier, srvCreated, srvModified, insertion_ordinal every other field
    • Indexes: the sizing experiment used keys for tenant ordinal, window reads, history and identifier. Additional index removals require evidence of use and query plans. Index decision
    • Measured on scratch copies of the 14-day rows:
    • entries 146 MB against 721 MB;
    • treatments 139 MB against 464 MB.
    • Value and query fidelity. In the synthetic HTTP corpus at official/dev 79144cbd0, two fresh MongoDB sites produced equivalent results across 27 observations (reproduced). A v1 numeric date-range query returned seven entries. V3 history returned all eight, including a string-valued date. V1 rendered a string-valued insulin field as a number while v3 history preserved its original string. The comparison also caught float32 rounding, a lost null, and a lost nested field. Retained evidence: stage1-storage-oracle-20261123, reference-responses.json and controls.log.
    • Keep an original value in rest whenever promoting it to a column would discard a distinction visible on these paths, including missing versus null. Numeric filter pushdown must preserve membership. Preserve sysTime and dateString when they differ from the strings that date derives. Measure the lossless implementation's footprint.

    Stage 2 — device status by tenant-hour¶

    One row per (tenant, hour) holding that hour's device-status documents as one JSON text value. The row passes the compression threshold, so documents share redundancy, the way MongoDB's block compression works. A slim identity index maps _id and identifier to the chunk.

    • Measured on the 14-day rows: 187 MB as JSON text with lz4, against 825 MB for the body-only layout.
    • Not chosen: jsonb with lz4 (216 MB), and jsonb with pglz (476 MB), because pglz gives up when the first 1 KB is incompressible.
    • Writes: the hour being written is the open chunk, rewritten on each upload of about 12 documents per hour.
    • Updates and deletes: a device-status update or delete rewrites its chunk. Read-derived: uploaders rarely update device status; this needs a count from a real-shaped corpus.

    The first adapter on core a5402ca99 points to the complexity cost (read-derived). It presents a document view over the chunks, maintains identity and time bounds, rewrites chunks in triggers, and prunes the chunks read for a query. The scratch byte measurement preceded these structures. A fresh-install design should select one representation per collection after measuring the real reader, writer and restore paths. Historical lab fixtures alone do not justify maintaining two storage implementations.

    Arrival-order replay at two history depths. With 100 tenants over two days and 76,504 device-status inserts, the implemented chunks consumed 224.1 MB after ordinary vacuum versus 174.8 MB for the cleaned document layout (reproduced). The chunk table had 4,900 inserts and 71,604 updates. Its identity table stored 76,504 key-to-location rows in 16.2 MB. These rows support lookup and uniqueness, and contain no copy of the document body. After a diagnostic full table rewrite, the two relations occupied 31.6 MB and 12.4 MB respectively. At fourteen days and 535,444 inserts, ordinary-vacuum sizes reached 726.1 MB with chunks versus 1,230.0 MB with the cleaned row layout, a 41.0% saving (reproduced). The identity table accounted for 115.2 MB. Each replay used one transaction per document as the serving role. The short seed and the deeper seed give different rates of space reuse. An alternative worth comparing writes each open-hour document once and seals the hour into a compressed chunk once, preserving immediate reads and late changes. Retained evidence: devicestatus-chunks-20261123/bytes/d2s2{b,c}.json, d2s14{b,c}.json and full-2d.txt.

    Stages 1 and 2 together¶

    About 497 MB for 100 tenants over 14 days, 0.36 MB per tenant-day (read-derived total of three measured scratch relations plus an estimated 25 MB for identity lookup). MongoDB on the same documents: snappy 0.45, zstd 0.30 MB per tenant-day, including per-database overhead.

    The model is database MB = 12.3 + 0.44 × provisioned tenants + daily MB × retained tenant-days. A retained tenant-day represents one day's history at the fixture's write rate. An inactive site's retained history still counts.

    • Original, 1.938 MB/day. The slope came from 100 tenants at two and 14 days. The model predicted 44.05 GB for the original 10k short-history restore, which measured 44.24 GB. This validates that layout and workload.
    • Candidate 2, about 1.61 MB/day. Apply the matched two-day clinical byte ratio (309,108,736 / 373,006,336) to the original slope. The earlier 391 GB forecast at 10k used a more optimistic scratch estimate of cleanup savings. A second history depth through the candidate's API would measure the new slope directly.
    • Device-status stage alone, about 0.88 MB/day. The original entries and treatments slopes were 0.510 and 0.330 MB/day. Scale each by its two-day cleaned-to-original table-size ratio (about 0.864 and 0.820). Add 0.166 MB/day for device status, from 187 MB of scratch chunks plus the estimated 25 MB identity index across 1,400 tenant-days and a 10% incremental-growth allowance. The implemented chunk metadata, indexes and live rewrites still need a product-path measurement.
    • Both stages, about 0.39 MB/day. Apply the same 10% allowance to the combined scratch estimate. At 10k tenants this gives 59 GB at 14 days, 121 GB at 30 days, 355 GB at 90 days and 1.43 TB at 365 days. Longer horizons extrapolate the same mix, write rate and compression. Retention remains a separate product decision.

    For a 10k, 30-day primary, the proposed 200 GiB volume is plausible only under the combined layout forecast. 121 GB is about 113 GiB. Allowing 32 GiB for WAL and keeping 25% of the volume free gives about 193 GiB. The reserve is an illustrative planning assumption. Peak WAL can exceed its target. With those same assumptions the chunk-only forecast needs about 375 GiB and candidate 2 about 646 GiB. Size backup storage and simultaneous restores separately from the serving primary. The dedicated-node measurements still determine latency and throughput.

    For 1,600 tenants each keeping three years, 1,095 days per tenant gives 1,752,000 retained tenant-days. The read-derived database estimates are 3.40 TB on the original layout, 2.81 TB on candidate 2, 1.54 TB with device-status chunks and 684 GB with both stages. Under the same WAL and free-space assumptions the combined layout needs about 892 GiB of primary volume, which fits a 1 TiB primary. Every 0.10 MB change in the measured daily footprint changes the three-year database estimate by 175 GB.

    Working full-history forecast using the implemented chunk writer: 1.13–1.30 TB of database data for those 1,600 tenants, assuming the typed entries and treatments achieve their projected 0.224 MB/day. The lower scenario starts at the measured fourteen-day footprint and extends it at the two-to-fourteen-day incremental rate ((726,097,920 − 224,116,736) / 1,200 = 0.418 MB per additional tenant-day). The other scenario carries the fourteen-day average of 0.519 MB/day through all three years. These are two extrapolations from the same synthetic replay. With the WAL and free-space allowances above they imply 1.41–1.62 TiB of primary volume. For 10,000 tenants at 30 days the same methods give 211–227 GB of database data and 305–325 GiB of primary volume. A longer steady workload and uploader mixes measure drift in either direction. The earlier 684 GB and 121 GB figures describe the compact targets for the two installations.

    If the typed entries and treatments qualify first, the cleaned device-status table can continue to serve the full history. In the arrival-order replay it grew from 174,817,280 to 1,229,971,456 bytes across 1,200 additional tenant-days, or 0.879 MB per tenant-day (reproduced, devicestatus-chunks-20261123/bytes/d2s14b.json). Adding the projected 0.224 MB/day for typed entries and treatments gives a 1.93 TB three-year fallback for 1,600 tenants. This estimate assumes the typed footprint and preserves the same retention.

    Where a three-year installation could save more¶

    At the fixture cadence of about 852 documents per tenant-day, this installation would retain about 1.49 billion entries, treatments and device-status documents. The 684 GB forecast averages roughly 460 bytes per document, including indexes. On the same documents the measured single-site MongoDB growth rates of 0.38 and 0.24 MB per tenant-day imply about 666 GB with Snappy and 420 GB with zstd in three years of incremental data and indexes. These are comparisons at this uploader mix. WiredTiger compresses records together in blocks and shares key prefixes within indexes. MongoDB 8.0 storage engine

    Keep every document. Qualify a single representation for each collection first. The scratch typed entries and treatments had 89.3 MB and 60.9 MB of indexes respectively. Those indexes alone were about 30% of the combined 497 MB layout. Their query plans, use during representative API traffic, and identity and ordering obligations determine which can be combined. The 684 GB forecast already assumes the smaller index set.

    For deeper savings, TimescaleDB keeps recent data in rows and converts older time chunks to compressed columnar storage. An equivalent storage experiment here keeps 30 days in the combined layout and groups older complete documents by tenant, collection and day. Lane R measured 26.8 MB of cold chunk tables over 200 tenant-days. At 1,600 tenants this gives 248 GB for hot data, cold bodies and fixed costs, before the cold lookup and history structures. A historical correction, an identity-based resend, an export spanning the boundary and a restore interrupted during compaction all need to return the same documents in the same order. Retained evidence: HANDOFF-storage-alternatives-20261123.md. TimescaleDB chunks and columnstore

    A prepaid PostgreSQL 16.15 scratch run priced one possible cold lookup table. Its unique key covered tenant, collection, identity kind and lookup value. Its row stored the destination day. Compactly encoding canonical ObjectId and UUID strings while preserving arbitrary UTF-8 keys reduced incremental table-plus-index bytes by 17% at one lookup row per document. All lookup tuples decoded to their original values; a variant that lowercased opaque keys lost information. Two depths provided the incremental rates, with 327,168 synthetic documents at the larger depth. At one lookup row per cold document, the three-year estimate for hot data, cold bodies and this table becomes 477 GB with packed keys or 524 GB with text. At one lookup per ten documents the corresponding projections are 271 GB or 277 GB. These examples use the compact target for the 30 hot days. Pricing that month with the implemented chunk writer adds 14–17 GB to each example, bringing the one-packed-key case to 491–494 GB. Both lookup frequencies are illustrative. Some documents carry two lookup keys. Measure the actual frequency, order/history metadata, changes to old records, and live WAL before sizing volumes for this design. Retained evidence: deep-history-locator-20261123/sizes.csv and locator.sql.

    Choose a shorter device-status history. Keeping entries and treatments for three years but device status for 30, 90 or 365 days projects 416–419, 456–468 or 640–697 GB respectively with the implemented chunk writer and proposed typed layout. The more compact scratch target projects 402, 418 or 491 GB. Older device-status uploads, including predictions and uploader state, disappear from historical API reads and exports. Nightscout already exposes an optional collection-specific retention setting in single-site mode. A default or an operator option for the multitenant service is a product decision. Nightscout configuration

    Not proposed¶

    • Typed device status (predictions as int2[], enacted object deduplicated). It measured worse: 1,026 MB against 825 MB, because smaller bodies fall under the compression threshold.
    • Filesystem compression (ZFS). Lane R's proxy measured 4.5×, but it saves disk only. The buffer cache and vacuum still see uncompressed pages, so it does not address the 10k symptoms. An operator may still choose it.
    • lz4 or toast_tuple_target alone. No effect below the threshold (measured).
    • Per-tenant-day cold chunks for every collection (lane R's candidate B, 0.13 MB per tenant-day for the cold part). It needs compaction, reads across both representations and history rules for moving rows. For multi-year installations, the scenarios above price the potential benefit. The measured open-chunk rewrite cost and the number of keys each uploader needs are inputs to that choice.

    Beyond the row layout: extensions and storage by age¶

    This proposal changes how one row is stored. The alternatives that change the database or store data by age are evaluated in the storage tiering proposal: - native time partitioning and its partition key (section 5.1–5.2); - cold monthly bundles, which revisit candidate B above for data older than a tenant-chosen boundary (5.4); - TimescaleDB, Citus, columnar and Parquet extensions, filesystem compression, BRIN indexes, dictionary compression and reports on a standby, each with predicted impact on bytes, the clinical path, long-range reads and operations (section 7).

    TimescaleDB is not proposed there because row-level security is not supported on its compressed chunks or under its continuous aggregates (vendor-documented), which conflicts with d3.

    5. What must not change¶

    • The API contract. v1 and v3 return each stored document with its unknown fields, and the same value a single site returns. The current jsonb layout already normalises key order and number representation. So the bar is the value equality today's differential tests check against official/dev, not byte identity of the upload (read-derived; the differential decides).
    • Identity: _id is the hub key and identifier the client sync identity (d20). Re-send outcomes match official/dev (multitenant-write-settings-at-defaults).
    • Queries: the closed operator set of d8 runs over typed columns plus rest, with mingo as the oracle. Filters on untyped fields evaluate on rest, correct but slower, as non-indexed filters are today.
    • Order and history: reads keep the insertion-ordinal tie-break (view-reads-total-order). v3 history never skips a late commit (write-clock-history-arms). A device-status chunk rewrite stamps srvModified on the changed document only.
    • The hub records, never derives dosing state (d18). No field is recomputed, stripped or summarised in storage.
    • Single-tenant MongoDB is unchanged (d4).
    • Schema changes in place until an operator release (prerelease-schema-in-place).

    Fresh installs and existing lab fixtures¶

    Read-derived at core 1aaa5af33: nine candidate catalogue files across six clinical domains still carry both 0099_*_storage_bytes (ten statements to install and populate the statistic) and 0100_*_storage_bytes_removed (three statements to remove it). Standard and socket-order catalogues are alternatives. Some initial migrations also build indexes that 0101 drops. The volume model assigns no daily savings to removing obsolete bootstrap steps.

    Reproduced on PostgreSQL 16.15 using the frozen migration runner, with a disposable database for each layout. Omitting the 0099 and 0100 pair in each of the six selected catalogues reduced the number of applied migrations from 41 to 29 and removed 78 SQL statements from the fresh bootstrap. Both sets passed migration checks. Schema-only dumps including policies, grants and indexes matched, as did the synthetic row returned after a write in each domain. An added index made the comparison fail and the original result returned after the control was removed. The old path left one dropped column slot per clinical table. The shorter path left zero.     7. Separate decisions this proposal does not take¶

    • Device-status prediction retention. Most projects keep predictions briefly. Single-site Nightscout offers a 30-day devicestatus delete. A retention policy is a product decision taken the same way for single-tenant and multitenant sites; storage stays agnostic.
    • Deep history beyond 30 days (multi-year tenants). Revisit candidate B after stage 1 and 2 measurements.

    9. Corrections from the stage 2 build (2026-11-24, measured by lane D2)¶

    • Read cost moves to PostgreSQL. Unpacking chunks made the evaluator window read 2–3.4× slower at 1,000 tenants (p95 82.3 → 113.3 ms, and 276.3 ms when filtering stored json text, which re-reads each document per field access). v1 and v3 reads became 2–4× faster.
    • Writes rewrite the hour. Per-document appends cost 2.2× WAL and leave dead chunk copies until vacuum, so a fresh 2-day load was 16 % larger than the current layout; the steady-state saving per added day was 82 %. A one-statement batch append made WAL linear and smaller than the current layout.
    • The identity index costs about 172 B per document, not the ~25 MB per 100 tenants estimated in section 4.
    • GIN on arrays cannot serve lookups under row-level security: the array operators are not leakproof, so they are never index conditions. The working design uses a btree on (tenant_id, key).
    • d11 (body retained as JSONB) would need marking superseded for multitenant device status if a chunk layout were ever adopted.
    • Untried: a small per-document header (created_at, isValid, srvModified, _id) beside the text, so the window filters without reading documents. Any revisit starts there.

    Scalar queries that can ship independently¶

    Reproduced. A 48-hour synthetic SGV query returned 576 rows in identical date/ordinal order with the same digest for ID kind, ID, date and SGV. Full JSONB fetched and projected in Node took 214,286 PostgreSQL DataRow bytes. Selecting the four columns took 40,885 bytes on each layout, an 80.9% reduction. Median client times over five rotated warm reads were 8.48 ms for the current document query, 3.65 ms for the current generated columns and 6.22 ms for the new typed columns. The 24-hour result showed the same reduction in bytes. The database ran under RLS on a prepaid worker alongside another test job. These are database-to-Node results for canonical numeric SGVs.

    Read-derived opportunities. The ordinary reader selects id_kind, doc and applies projections in Node (lib/storage/postgres-entries-read.js). Its existence-only writer callers already declare an _id projection. They can use a narrow query with the same identity, scope and sort contracts. For the operator's provisioning and restore floor check, lib/server/serve/postgres-domains.js currently extracts the maximum numeric srvModified from the document across all selected domains. The ordered entries and treatments expose the numeric column. Checking the same bound on that column is a candidate for avoiding full-document work during commissioning, while preserving the single database snapshot and the fallback for other domains. The evaluator can also select modification stamps and identity first when it decides which rows a resident projection needs to reload. Its existing write clock and full-snapshot fallback still own ordering and recovery. A reporting or chart query that consumes only time and glucose can reuse the existing generated columns when its field semantics are explicit. Plugins, exports and full-document API routes retain lossless documents. Validate any scalar substitution against the current behavior on missing, null, differently typed and extension fields. The retained comparison is lab/scalar-window-pilot.json.

    Use the existing index set for the first measurements. On PostgreSQL, fetching a subset of columns reduces transfer even when a heap visit is required. A covering index can also avoid heap reads when its payload covers the query and the visibility map permits it.     What depends on insertion order¶

    Read-derived. A fresh design can use a document's typed identity to settle ties after the requested sort keys. That would change the evaluator contract. The SQL and in-process paths currently use insertion order, and earlier work observed different evaluator output on tied documents. Compare output from both paths on equal-time entries carrying different values and reversed ID order before making that choice. Evaluator order decision

    The public v3 history URL resumes strictly after a timestamp. Its header alternative rounds to seconds and includes the boundary. Neither accepts the last ID of a page. When timestamps can tie, continuing inside a group requires carrying the full key across requests. An opaque cursor can encode the modification stamp, ID kind and stored ID for one collection. The tenant write clock separately orders committed writes so a later commit cannot appear behind a client's watermark. The four limited-page probes above checked tie choices within a page. See core lib/api3/generic/history/operation.js and lib/storage/postgres-write-clock.js.

    Reproduced in the v1 resend parity corpus. A resend without a unique identity can match two entries on the same sysTime and type. The earlier stored entry receives the update even when it carries the higher ID. The response can identify the last match. Treatment fallback first uses the existing identifier and date priority, then arrival order for the remaining ties. These cases can arise on fresh installations. See core tests/v1-resend-official-parity.integration.test.js and lib/storage/treatment-key-order.js.

    Read-derived from measured relation sizes. On the 93,961-document typed fixture, the two unique ordinal indexes occupy 6,103,040 B. The counter relations including their own indexes occupy 516,096 B. Together they account for 6.65% of the 99,475,456 B for these two collections. Net savings across the database require a comparative load with the replacement access paths. The retained inputs are lab/index-sizes.json and lab/frozen-size.json.

    Design option. Preserve the legacy selection and reply behavior only for ambiguous groups, or explicitly choose a new winner for future v1 writes. Test a second resend, deletion of the current winner and a restore before repricing a design without the per-row ordinal. Across hot and cold data, the public sort and limit still select one ordered result, and sync must traverse edits and deletions. Late uploads and lookups by ID or identifier can still require a per-document location. Compare the same fixture in a single table and split across tiers, with an equal-key pair on opposite sides. The counter and restore decision remains in force.
