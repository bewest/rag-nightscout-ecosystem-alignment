    Claimed public names, the alias registry and the deploy companion: scope split and line of sight¶

    Record

    Record as of 2026-11-09. A point-in-time document; it is not kept up to date.

    Contributor-facing decision-preparation note, 2026-11-09, read-derived. Anchors: alignment main 9029fb6dd; cgm-remote-monitor draft #8898 candidate ed2ced00; nightscout-roles-gateway 90840ace; nightscout-gateway-www 67cbe466. No service was run for this note. It proposes a scope split between cgm-remote-monitor, a sibling deployment repository and hoster-private adapters, and an ordered path to a running staging rehearsal. The sibling repository exists as a local scaffold at ~/src/nightscout-deploy (also reachable as externals/work/nightscout-deploy in this workspace); it has no remote yet, so the GitHub links in its README resolve only after the maintainer publishes it. Nothing here is adopted until it is a row in release decisions; the public-name contract and the production critical path keep their canonical roles.

    1. The model to carry forward: a claimed public name¶

    The roles gateway already separates the three things the public-name contract asks for, in a schema that ran unmodified against current Kratos in the Ory proof §5. Read at 90840ace:

    roles-gateway concept (table / column) what it is tenancy vocabulary it maps to
    registered_sites.expected_name (unique) the host label a person claims and may publish permanent public alias, owner-chosen
    registered_sites.upstream_origin where the edge sends an admitted request private internal name (a resolution label in the shared cohort, not a URL)
    registered_sites.owner_ref the identity-provider subject who owns the claim tenant owner, current OIDC subject
    reserved_expected_names, reserved_upstream_origin names nobody may claim, with a reason and source project floor plus operator list
    nightscout_authenticity_records proof that the claimant controls the upstream, checked out of band implied by provisioning in multitenant; needed only when fronting an independent site
    registered_sites.is_enabled, require_identities, exempt_matching_api_secret per-site policy switches alias policy binding: anonymous allowed, human login required, native device credential path (D17)
    group_definitions, group_inclusion_specs, connection_policies, scheduled_policies, joined_groups who is in a group, what a group may do, when the group/schedule policy the public-name contract defers to a bounded decision
    warden route GET/HEAD /warden/v1/active/backend/for/:expected_name one auth_request decision returning x-upstream-origin and an exchanged token the edge decision point; in the cohort the decision moves inside the admitted application

    What the model adds beyond the gateway: a stable tenant ID that none of these tables has (expected_name is the primary handle throughout), and an explicit alias kind with a lifetime. Two kinds are enough for the first production scope:

    • claimed: permanent, owner-chosen, checked against the reserved list, safe to publish; knowing it confers nothing, current policy decides.
    • ephemeral: issued by the operator or by an operator's adapter, rotatable or expiring, bound to the same tenant; expiry changes reachability, never ownership, and the name carries no capability (the public-name contract already says so).

    The public contract covers kinds, lifetimes, policy binding, versioned reassignment and cache invalidation. How an operator mints, distributes or brands ephemeral names is an adapter behind that contract, which is what keeps a hoster's own issuance scheme out of the public repositories without leaving a hole in the model.

    Default names, built in (direction given in discussion, 2026-11-09). Every tenant gets a generated label at provisioning: DNS-safe, lowercase, fixed length, drawn from a CSPRNG, starting with two letters so it is never confused with a number or an identifier, and checked against the reserved list. The label is the tenant's internal resolution name and, under the deployment's base domain, its default permanent primary alias. That is the whole built-in: a deployment with no issuer and no owner claims still has one working, unguessable name per tenant. The precedent is the per-site runner's own internal naming, which the maintainer found desirable in operation.

    Everything beyond the default is policy a hoster supplies behind core's contract: an alias issuer for ephemeral or additional stable names, and an alias policy that decides whether an owner may claim or choose a name (pattern, length, reserved list, cooling-off on release). An owner claim goes through the owner API under that policy; on rename the previous label stays as a stable secondary alias until the owner releases it, so configured uploaders keep working; no timer is involved.

    What does not carry forward, from what the port measured and what the tables hold:

    • registered_sites.api_secret and nightscout_secrets.api_secret hold a site's root credential in clear, because the gateway verifies authenticity and brokers tokens with it. In the cohort the tenant's root lives in the secrets component, and owner authority treats legacy hashes as bearer credentials. The registry stores no credential.
    • upstream_origin as a URL. A shared cohort has no per-tenant origin; resolution ends at a stable tenant ID inside the admitted application (D10, D13, D14).
    • The runtime: restify on Node 24 fails at require, the lockfile needs --legacy-peer-deps, the per-site Hydra client path needs reconfiguration (Ory proof §5). The schema vocabulary, the decision chain's cases including its own cross-site denial, and the WWW journeys (what I own, why I have access, what I consent to, how I leave) are the inheritance. The code is reference, not a dependency.

    2. Scope split: core, sibling repository, hoster-private¶

    The rule: core owns anything that defines authority or identity; the sibling owns anything that varies by hoster or vendor; a hoster owns only adapters behind published interfaces.

    concern cgm-remote-monitor sibling deploy repository (working name nightscout-deploy) hoster-private
    Private names: stable tenant ID and internal resolution name owns: migration under the project's runner, operator API on the private plane consumes snapshots —
    Public aliases: host, kind, lifetime, policy binding, reserved names owns the record and the decision: the same migration, the operator API, and a read-only resolution endpoint on the private plane returning tenant ID, policy and a snapshot version the auth_request facade that calls that endpoint and caches it, for edges that resolve before proxying; a reference issuer for ephemeral aliases a replacement facade or issuer behind the same endpoint
    Resolver owns lib/tenant-resolution; adds a snapshot-source interface (the README already says "construct a new resolver to replace the mapping") registry-backed snapshot source, cache, invalidation signal —
    Edge owns the trust predicate contract (peer list) and the fast unknown-host refusal in the app reference nginx, auth_request adapter for edges that need one, trust-record template, spoof/conflict/outage probes TLS termination, DNS, certificates
    Alias policy: anonymous vs human-required vs device path owns the binding and the decision point on every exposed path — —
    Group and schedule policy later, after the bounded decision the public-name contract requires later: the WWW journeys as an adapter over the headless owner API —
    Ephemeral alias issuance the kind, lifetime and reassignment rules a reference issuer for the Compose and Kubernetes bindings a hoster's own issuer and distribution
    Human identity generic OIDC adapter, conformance cases provider configuration profiles (Authentik reference, Kratos fallback); no login UI the provider deployment
    Owner self-service one thin page over the headless owner API — branding, support links
    Operator lifecycle headless operator API and lifecycle library (exists at ed2ced00) CLI, console (read-mostly first), runbooks billing, ticketing
    Secrets component contract and the Vault Transit reference provider cloud key-service providers, service-login adapters, conformance kit key custody
    Deployment the deployment-plan schema and the role table plan renderer; Compose, Kubernetes and DIY single-tenant bindings; targets registry with owner and expiry per vendor derived image with installed providers
    Observability readiness and admission endpoints shaped for fleet polling Grafana mixin, alert rules, runbook skeletons paging

    Why the alias record is core even though an edge may resolve it first (answered in discussion with the maintainer, 2026-11-09): the application must refuse an unknown host and apply the alias policy on every exposed path, sockets included, so it needs the alias record inside its own authority. The resolver already accepts X-Resolved-Tenant only as a consistency check against its own map. An edge that resolves is therefore a cache in front of core's record, and the sibling ships the demo facade for it; a hoster can swap the facade without touching authority. Deployments with a plain edge (Compose, DIY) use the same record in direct-alias mode and need no facade.

    Two placement rules from operating experience, stated as mechanisms:

    1. Config names a registered provider; it never names a module path. A module path in config is code execution for anyone who can write config and it hides the supply chain from the image digest. Providers are installed at image build and registered by name; core refuses unknown names.
    2. The trusted-peer predicate is configuration, not a provider. It is the one place where a wrong adapter silently widens trust.

    3. Line of sight to a staging rehearsal¶

    Waves show dependency, not dates. Each wave names the gate that ends it. "Rehearsal" means a synthetic export of a few tenants loaded into the Compose binding on a clean host, served through a TLS edge with a written trust record, and restored once, with nothing real in it.

    wave core sibling gate
    0 (in flight) ed2 one-host reference accepted; shared OIDC/owner source accepted natively; Vault service login behind the existing seam — M6 acceptance families for identity and secrets
    1 registry migration, operator API, snapshot export; resolver snapshot source; unknown-host refusal before tenant context; reserved-name floor repository created; plan schema extracted from deploy/multitenant/config.js; resolver and secrets conformance kits with red controls registry CRUD plus alias rename, reassignment and revocation measured on PostgreSQL; a stale snapshot refuses, a fresh one serves
    2 full Mongo→PostgreSQL loader for the admitted collections (CAP-02); operator CLI verbs over the headless API Compose binding consumes deploy/multitenant at a pinned commit; edge reference with probes; trust-record template; targets registry empty but checked loader round-trip on synthetic exports with populated identity and order fidelity; CLI provision/suspend/export/delete/bind-alias against the Compose binding
    3 — staging rehearsal on Compose: TLS edge, provider over HTTPS, Vault service login, backup and restore of the assembled tree, upgrade and rollback the deployment admission probes pass against the live edge and Vault; one restore rehearsed
    4 thin owner page Kubernetes binding (one Pod per cohort, private socket volume, no-overlap update); console read-mostly; first two DIY single-tenant targets with expiry dates binding equivalence table in the public-name contract
    5 group/schedule policy after its decision packet WWW journeys over the owner API; connector sync tooling; observability mixin policy evidence list in the public-name contract

    Priority between the three columns: core first where it defines authority (waves 1 and 2 are mostly core), the sibling where it lowers the cost of running a deployment (waves 3 and 4), and hoster-private work only as adapters once the interface they sit behind exists.

    4. Questions for the maintainer, one topic each¶

    Items marked answered in discussion were settled with the maintainer on 2026-11-09 and await their row in release decisions; the rest are open.

    1. Where deploy/multitenant lives during 16.0.0. Answered in discussion: re-running acceptance to move a directory is process drag, so the move is not gated on it. Move the tree into the sibling now with its history (git subtree split), record the source commit, and make the sibling's check a byte-identity comparison of the moved tree against that pinned core commit plus the existing demo.sh smoke. Core keeps a one-paragraph pointer. The ed2 evidence keeps its attribution because the files it cites are unchanged, only relocated.
    2. Registry authority. Answered in discussion: private names are core schema. Public aliases are also a core record with a read-only private resolution endpoint; the sibling ships the auth_request facade over it and a hoster may replace the facade. Mechanism in §2.
    3. Default alias kind and label length. Answered in discussion: a simple built-in default (§1). The generated label's length is provisional at 10 characters (two letters plus eight from a 32-symbol lowercase alphabet, about 40 bits) and is to be measured against collision at the intended tenant counts and against typing and reading comfort before it is fixed. Uploaders, followers and controllers are configured with one hostname and do not rotate it, so a tenant needs exactly one permanent primary alias. Whether that name is owner-chosen or operator-generated (unguessable) is a separate axis from its lifetime. Ephemeral aliases are additive and never the only alias; reassignment inherits nothing. Recommendation: default one permanent primary alias per tenant, generated unless claimed; ephemeral aliases opt-in per tenant under operator policy.
    4. Reserved names as a project floor. A built-in list (well-known, api, admin, www and the like) plus an operator list, in the D22 style. Recommendation: yes.
    5. Roles gateway reuse. Port or re-implement. Recommendation: re-implement the decision chain over core's resolver and current authority, carry the schema vocabulary and the gateway's test cases, credit the design; the measured port cost is the runtime and dependency graph.
    6. Strong identity for single and multitenant, and the gateway's disposition. Direction given in discussion: both profiles should have strong human identity, and uploaders may move toward zero trust over the long term. The adopted generic OIDC adapter already runs in single under ENABLE=oidc, so fronting an independent site with an external gateway is not needed for human identity; the gateway is archived as reference once the decision chain and the group and schedule vocabulary are carried into core. For uploaders, D17's permanent native credential path stays; a later contract can add per-device enrolment through the owner API, with short-lived or attested device tokens and the legacy secret as the compatibility path. That is a core identity contract; the sibling would hold enrolment tooling only. No change to D17 is proposed here.
    7. Retiring sole maintenance of the roles gateway while OIDC lands in both profiles. Feasible, and the order matters more than the effort. Conditions: (i) the gateway serves no production users; the maintainer's operator reports a staging-only deployment; (ii) the generic OIDC adapter is accepted natively in single and multitenant, which is wave 0; (iii) a minimal membership record exists in core, a person bound to a tenant with a role and revocable on the next operation, so the question the gateway answered, who may act on this site, has a home. The identity plan (MT-IDENTITY-PLAN) already names tenant access grants. Groups and schedules stay deferred to their bounded decision; nothing in retirement depends on them. Steps: archive the repository with a pointer to the alignment note, keep its test cases as the source of the resolver and OIDC conformance kits, and port the WWW journeys later as a sibling adapter over the owner API. For single, answered in discussion: an OIDC subject becomes a Nightscout subject by explicit binding by the site admin, with an invitation flow later; no auto-provisioning on first login. In addition, when OIDC is enabled an environment variable may name the one subject that binds to the site's admin at boot, so a fresh site can be claimed without a console; the mechanism and its refusal rules (unset, malformed, already bound) need a small decision row of their own.
    8. Repository home and licence for the sibling. Answered in discussion: the nightscout organisation, as nightscout-deploy. Licence recommendation: AGPL-3.0-only, matching cgm-remote-monitor and nightscout-connect and required anyway by the imported Compose tree, with the provider and plan interface contracts published as a small separate MIT package (the roles gateway's licence) so a hosting operator's private providers are not derivative works. The workspace lockfile carries the intended URL; bootstrap reports a failed clone until the repository is published, and the local scaffold stands in meanwhile.

    Draft for maintainer review. Decisions taken go into decisions.md as rows; the queue and the public-name contract move only after the row exists.
